Aligning pork legs to the trotter saw, and the skill library it starts

A leg arrives on the belt at any yaw. The cell turns it so the hock rides the saw's blade plane and the trotter hangs off the open edge. Each step is a skill with a contract. The same skills, and the compositions between them, are how the rest of the plant gets automated.

What this plan proposes
  1. Cell: overhead depth camera, one arm behind the far rail, a hold-down belt at the saw. The arm turns the leg on the belt without lifting it, unless the experiment in 7.1 shows lifting works better.
  2. Task: perceive the leg, choose where to intercept it, grasp it, orient it so the hock sits on the blade plane, release before the hold-down, verify. Every outcome of every skill is routed to a next skill or a terminal; the graph refuses to build otherwise.
  3. Deterministic first: depth keypoints, IK and contract checks are classical. Learning goes where the tests fail: the orient manoeuvre under leg-to-leg variation, and perception if keypoints miss their gate.
  4. Composition: skills are distributions, and there are three ways to compose them. Combining actions promises something about each step; combining specifications promises the outcome, exactly only under deterministic dynamics; fusing inputs promises only the belief. Each skill in the cell is assigned one, and its contract adds the detector for what that type cannot promise (4.3).
  5. Wednesday demo: one learned orient skill, run unchanged on a SCARA and a six-axis arm, acting in the belt frame through each arm's own IK. The SCARA run exceeds the SR-20iA's published wrist inertia and is labelled as such; the manoeuvre choice is Lakshya's (6.2).
  6. Plant: the same primitive skills (perceive, track, intercept, grasp, turn, place, hold, cut, verify) compose into the other manual jobs on the line; section 8 ranks which cell comes next by skills reused.
Why an arm, when a bar would do

If the job were only this one step, squaring a leg for the saw, the simplest answer is a fixed bar across the belt, angled against the flow: every leg that meets it turns toward one heading, with no camera and no program. The hold-down in section 1 is already half of that fixture, and a guide-bar trial on the line would settle in an afternoon whether a leg with a hanging trotter behaves as well as a box does † not tried.

The leg station is not the goal. The goal is a physical intelligence for the plant: a system that adapts to the job in front of it by composing the skills it has into the sub-goals the job needs, that takes in new skills as we write or teach them, and that can make a new skill for itself by composing two it already has. A footballer learns running and learns dribbling, often in different places and at different times; dribbling at a run is a third skill neither session taught, made from the two, and in most cases the two do not get in each other's way. Sections 3 to 5 and 13 are about giving the cell that property, and the leg is where it is first built and judged, on the simplest job the line has, with a saw as the referee. The arm is what makes the skills portable to the next job, which a bar is not.

Section 1

The task and the cell

Legs come down a conveyor belt at random angles. Before they reach a saw at the end of the belt, each leg has to be turned so its foot sticks out over the edge, with the hock joint exactly where the blade will cut. We built a computer simulation of the whole station, with the belt, the saw and a robot arm, so we can try ideas safely and measure how well each one works before touching real hardware.

Neil set alignment as the first task, starting with the simpler version of the problem. At the leg station today, workers turn whole bone-in legs one-handed so the trotter reaches the open right-hand edge of the belt. Legs arrive touching, at anything from square to about 45 degrees off. Further down the belt, a circular saw cuts the trotter off at the hock. The cell goes into Prestage Foods of Iowa near Eagle Grove.

What aligned means, in numbers the saw can judge. The sim does not score alignment by eye. The saw scores it: when the blade surface meets the leg surface the cut starts, the blade pushes back with a feed resistance, and when the leg has travelled its width the cut completes and reports the offset from the hock at entry and exit and the angle off square. The pre-registered pass is within ±10 mm and ±5 degrees, both † assumed, since no tolerance has come from the customer.

Section 3

Skills with contracts, tasks as graphs

We break the job into small robot skills: find the leg, grab it, turn it, let go. Every skill states up front what it needs, what it promises to deliver, and every way it can fail. Skills are chained into a task, and for each possible failure the task says what happens next, so the robot never ends up without a plan.

We are building an architecture where a skill can be deterministic motion, classical control, optimisation, a learned policy or a perception module, and every skill carries the same contract: inputs, preconditions, outputs, success conditions, failure conditions and recovery. Tasks are graphs of skills, so a new application reuses most of the graph and adds only what is new about it. The library lives in src/robotics/core/skill_library/ and imports nothing from the simulator or about pork. Recovery belongs to the task graph, because what to do after a failure depends on the task.

3.1One contract, as it runs today

The runner does not trust the skill. Inputs are type-checked; a failing precondition stops the skill before it moves; a skill may end only in success or a failure it declared; and a claimed success is re-checked, and reported as success_not_verified if the checks disagree. Every check returns the number it decided on, so a failed episode explains itself in values.

3.2Where learning goes in this graph

We use deterministic methods wherever they are reliable, and learning where uncertainty, soft tissue, contact or variation make it necessary. In this graph that puts learning in two places, each used only after the deterministic version fails its gate:

  • orient: turning a leg on a belt is a contact task with belt friction, leg-to-leg shape variation and a trotter that drapes. A scripted rotation about the centre of gravity is the baseline; a learned policy, or a learned residual on it, replaces it only if the scripted one misses the saw tolerance on the 20-leg population.
  • locate_leg: hock and trotter keypoints from depth geometry first. The old slab estimator used the area centroid and was off by 47 mm and 19 degrees on a leg, because a leg's centroid is in the ham while the task is defined at the hock. A learned keypoint model enters only if geometry misses its gate, especially on touching legs.

The contract does not change when the implementation does. A learned orient skill declares the same inputs, preconditions, success checks and failure modes as a scripted one, and the graph around it does not know the difference.

Section 4

Composing skills: action, outcome, input

Once single skills work, we want to combine them into new ones, the way a football player combines running and dribbling. There are three ways to combine skills: blend what they do, combine what they are trying to achieve, or combine what they see. Each way comes with a different kind of guarantee. Combining skills can also produce surprises, good or bad, so every combination gets tested.

A skill is a distribution in two senses: over actions given what it observes, and over the trajectories and outcomes it produces when it runs. Composing skills is an operation on one of those distributions, or on the inputs that feed them. Which one decides what the composed skill can promise. Source: library/topics/skill-composition-action-outcome-input.md.

4.1Same two skills, two compositions, different behaviour

A corridor of states 0 to 8, goal states 0, 3 and 4, start at 5. Skill A wants goal 0 or 3; skill B wants 0 or 4. The composed task is A AND B: reach 0. Finishing at a desired goal pays +2, at another goal −2, every step −0.1. Deterministic, so the exactness conditions hold.

Outcome composition has limits too. In van Niekerk's counterexample with stochastic outcomes, max-composition picks an action worth 0.9 where the true OR optimum is 1.0; the lower bound still holds, but neither operator's choice is justified once the assumptions go. A wet leg sliding on a belt is not deterministic, so for this cell outcome composition gives bounds, and the saw measures the rest.

Input composition has its own quiet failure. Fusing a depth estimate of the hock (102 ± 4 mm) with a laser line (96 ± 2 mm) and a prior (100 ± 20 mm) gives 97.2 ± 1.8 mm; drop the laser and it gives 101.9 ± 3.9 mm, handled by construction. Bias the laser by +10 mm and the fused estimate moves 8 mm while the reported uncertainty stays at 1.8 mm: the product carries no signal that its experts disagree † illustrative sensor numbers.

4.2Non-additivity and emergence

Definition used here: a composition is additive when the composite's outcome equals the operator applied to the constituents' outcomes, so AND reaches exactly what both accept. The exactness theorems are exactly the cases where additivity is proven. Non-additivity is any departure; emergence is a departure into an outcome neither constituent produces, useful or harmful. Doing X and Y together gives the union of their outcomes only inside those theorems' assumptions.

Section 5

Skills as distributions in a latent space

A learned skill can be stored as a point in a space of skills, where nearby points behave alike. That would let us mix and adjust skills without teaching every variation from scratch. The research shows this only works under specific conditions, so for now we use the idea inside individual skills and keep each skill's checks outside it.

A learned skill is a distribution over actions given what the robot observes. Embedding skills in a shared latent space is what would let the library interpolate between them, add them, plan over them and carry them to another robot: running and dribbling as two points whose combination is a third skill neither demonstrated. The evidence says this works under specific conditions, and that no published latent carries a precondition or a success test. Source for this section: library/topics/latent-skill-representations.md, about 55 papers read in full.

5.1Adding skills in latent space works only when trained for it

A point between two skills can decode to an action nobody demonstrated. A latent that lies between two trained skills can decode to actions that appear nowhere in the data. On a 1-D walk whose data actions lie only in [−1, −0.8] and [0.8, 1], a Gaussian skill prior sampled in between, and the policy built on it averaged −66 reward against −2.2 with a diffusion prior (Venkatraman et al. 2023, App. D). On a real Sawyer, the mean of two push-skill latents "was not reliable for half of the synthetic goals" (Julian et al. 2018).

Collapse is measured in robot skill models. A skill VAE on CALVIN fell from 28.3 to 16.2 percent five-in-a-row success when β went from 0.01 to 0.1, "an over-regularized model which learns to ignore the latent plans" (Mees et al. 2022, 3 seeds). None of SPiRL, SkiLD, OPAL, CompILE, EXTRACT or QueST reports a β ablation.

Latents learned from video encode the wrong motion. Distractors raised a latent-action probe error from 2.55 to 22.09 (Nikulin et al., 4 environments × 3 seeds). On a belt every background pixel moves. † That camera-frame codes would encode belt travel is a prediction, untested.

The same composition gives different skills depending on how each skill's distribution is represented. Section 6

One learned skill, two arms

Tomorrow's demonstration shows one taught skill, turning a leg on the belt, running unchanged on two very different robots: a SCARA arm, which can only move flat and turn, and a six-joint arm. The skill describes how the leg should move, and each robot works out how to move its own joints to follow that. This is what lets the same skill run on both.

The Wednesday proof of concept: one learned orient skill, one checkpoint, run unchanged on the FANUC SR-20iA SCARA and the UR20 doing the same manoeuvre. It is the smallest real instance of skills that work across platforms. What makes it honest is stating exactly what transferred, and measuring it against what the arms alone lose.

6.1What the literature has and has not shown

Most "cross-embodiment" results are co-training: one model copes with mixed data from robots it was trained on. CrossFormer, trained on 20 embodiments, says it does "not yet show significant positive transfer" (Doshi et al. 2024, Sec. 5), and π0 never tests a robot held out of pretraining (Black et al. 2024). Every real result on an arm left out of training expresses actions as end-effector poses and relies on that arm's own IK and controller. No paper found transfers a policy to a four-axis arm or a SCARA † one search pass; an absence, not a result.

6.2The design

The only grasp both arms can make is the shank grasp from above with the tool vertical; the trotter end-on grasp needs tilt and the SCARA refuses it. With the tool vertical, the manoeuvre lives in x, y and yaw at a fixed height: the SCARA's native space, and a subset of the UR20's. So the policy acts in the leg's world, not the arm's, and neither arm appears in its training data.

Open decision

The two research notes behind this section disagree on which manoeuvre the shared skill should be, and the disagreement comes down to the SCARA's wrist.

  • Grasp and rotate on the belt (cross-embodiment-skill-transfer.md). The shank grasp from above already works on both arms. But turning a gripped leg accelerates its inertia about J4 whether or not the belt carries its weight, and that is 1.06× the SR-20iA's limit through the centre of gravity and 4.06× on the shank.
  • Planar push, tool vertical (latent-skill-representations.md). The arm never carries the leg's inertia, so the SCARA stays inside its rating. But a point push does not repeat (3.4 to 11.7 mm spread in the MIT dataset), so it has to be a line contact against a datum, which the cell does not have yet.

Gripping through the centre of gravity is not available: it lies in the ham, about 250 mm wide on the default leg, and the jaw opens to 180 mm. The shank grasp is the one both arms share, and there the SCARA is several times over its rating. Recommendation: run Wednesday's demo as grasp-and-rotate, because it exists on both arms today, and label the SCARA run on screen as over its published J4 inertia and not deployable as shown. Build the line push against a datum as the SCARA's production candidate after Wednesday. Whether FANUC permits any inertia overshoot at reduced acceleration is † unverified, a question for FANUC.

Warning

The SCARA can succeed in simulation at a move the real arm cannot make. The default leg is 1.06× the SR-20iA's J4 inertia limit gripped through its centre of gravity and 4.06× gripped on the shank 350 mm away, and neither simulated arm has torque limits. That number goes on screen next to the SCARA run.

Other known risks: the leg slides instead of turning (friction is randomised for this reason); the arms sag under load differently (absolute pose targets, replanned at 10 Hz, absorb a steady lag, and Mirage's delta-action collapse is the evidence for absolute); MuJoCo's flat jaw holds 0.60 to 0.70 of its rating on a cylinder, so a slip may be the contact model. rll has no torch and the GPU driver is mismatched until a reboot, so training runs on CPU; its time is unmeasured.

Why the orient skill grips the leg. Pushing at a single point does not repeat on any arm. Section 8

From one cell to the plant

The leg station is the first of many jobs in the plant that people do by hand today. Most of those jobs reuse the same basic skills: seeing a piece, grabbing it, moving it, placing it. By counting which skills each job needs, we can choose next the stations that reuse the most of what we have already built.

Every manual job on a pork line is a skill or a composition of skills. The plan for the plant is to write each job as a graph over a shared library and count what it reuses. Three sourced facts set what automating a job is worth:

  • Pieces per minute drive injury risk. The FSIS-commissioned UCSF study (574 workers, six plants, July 2024 to January 2025) found the number of hog parts a worker handles per minute "more closely associated with MSD risk" than line speed (Federal Register 2026-03228). A cell that takes the pieces at one station removes the exposure that was measured.
  • Labour per line per shift. FSIS assumes plants running faster add 3 to 5 production workers per line per shift, costed at $38.62 per hour loaded (same document, regulatory impact analysis).
  • Yield outweighs labour. In the MLA analysis summarised in pork-processing-line.md section 8.4, about 15 percent of automation benefit was labour and 80 percent carcass value yield. The leg cell earns its keep by protecting the cut position.

Rate: US plants under NSIS on a line-speed waiver averaged 1,276 head per hour in February 2025 (same source). At 1,000 head per hour the cutting floor sees 2,000 legs per hour.

8.1Three layers: primitives, composed skills, jobs

Eighteen primitive skills cover every manipulation job in the inventory. 10.1The prediction and intercept maths, as built

The product is stuck to the belt, and an encoder measures belt travel s(t). So each camera frame k gives the product's position in belt coordinates, which stays constant while the product does not slip:

xbelt=xworld(tk)−s(tk)

Averaging those readings gives x‾belt, and the slope of the readings against time measures slip directly. The position at a future time is bookkeeping, with belt speed v from the encoder rather than from differencing images:

x^world(tf)=x‾belt+s(tlast)+v(tf−tlast)

The planner then finds the meeting time by repeating one step until it stops changing: the leg is wherever it will be when the arm arrives, and the arm needs its command delay τ plus its travel time to get there.

tmeet=tnow+τ+Tmove(x^world(tmeet)−xtool) Tmove(d)=2damax+vbeltamaxwhen d≤vmax2amax Tmove(d)=dvmax+vmaxamax+vbeltamaxotherwise

The travel time is a trapezoid speed profile with top speed vmax and acceleration amax, plus the ramp to match belt speed. A meeting is accepted only if the leg is still inside the arm's window after the jaws have closed.

What this does not handle yet: the belt changing speed. If it accelerates at a during the lead time Δt, the prediction is off by e=12aΔt2, which is 12.5 mm for every 0.1 m/s² over a 0.5 s lead † computed; the plant belt's speed changes are unmeasured. Replanning from fresh encoder readings during the approach shrinks Δt and with it that error.

10.2How each piece gets better

  1. Each stage has a fixed test set: the same legs, poses and belt speeds every time.

11.1Placing the depth camera and looking at what it sees

Before anything can find a leg, the cell needs eyes. We put a depth camera over the belt, 950 mm up, and looked at its raw feed: a colour image, and a depth image that says how far away every pixel is.

How the technology works. An RGB-D camera of this kind is two infrared cameras a fixed distance apart, plus a projector that throws a dot pattern onto the scene so even a plain white belt has texture. The camera finds the same dot in both images; how far it shifts between them gives the distance, the same way your two eyes judge depth. Because that shift gets smaller the further away something is, the depth error grows with the square of the distance: RMS = Z² × subpixel ÷ (focal length × baseline) (Intel). At our 0.95 m that works out to about 1.2 mm on the belt.

What we tested. Two real cameras modelled from their datasheets, the Orbbec Gemini 335L and the RealSense D455, run over all 20 simulated legs in 9 positions each. We checked the simulated lens against MuJoCo's renderer (0.3 px), and compared depth noise, how many pixels land on the shank, and whether the whole leg stays in view.

11.2Segmenting the leg in simulation: geometry, then learned

Next the cell has to cut the leg out of the picture: decide which pixels are leg and which are belt, rail or arm. Everything after this, the centre of gravity, the grip point and the intercept, reads from that outline.

The geometric method. A leg stands proud of the belt, so height finds it. We record the empty belt first, then for every pixel measure how far the surface has risen above that recording. Anything between 10 and 300 mm up, inside the belt area, is leg; we keep the largest piece and refuse the frame if it is empty or runs off the image. It needs no training and no labels, and every decision can be traced to a number.

What the first runs taught us. The first run cut off every trotter hanging past the belt edge, because out there the recording saw the floor, not the belt, so the trotter looked a metre tall. The fix measured height against the belt's plane instead, but the plane fit then locked onto the floor, which fills more of this wide camera's view than the belt does. Fitting only inside the belt fixed both. On the third run every one of 180 frames came out almost exactly right: 2 wrong pixels out of 67,526.

Why that is too good, and the edge effects. The simulator draws a perfectly sharp depth edge around the leg. A real stereo camera does not: near an outline it matches its little windows across the edge, so it reports depths from the wrong side, or a mixture of near and far, and it loses surfaces seen almost edge-on. We added those three effects to the camera model: about 1 px of sideways jitter at edges (what a RealSense D435 measures below 0.7 m, Halmetschlager-Funek et al. 2019, Table 2), a 2 px band either side of the outline where half the pixels report the near depth, the far depth or a blend † assumed, and no return from surfaces tilted past 75 degrees from the camera, where the published noise fit stops (Fankhauser et al. 2015; the cut-off itself † assumed). Then we measured again on the same 180 frames.

The learned method. A U-Net (Ronneberger et al. 2015): a network that shrinks the image step by step to understand what is where, then grows it back to full size to say, pixel by pixel, whether it is leg. Ours is small, about half a million parameters, reads the colour and depth images at 320 × 200, and is trained on 990 simulated frames of 90 legs, checked on 110 frames of 10 more, none of them among the 20 test legs. It runs on the CPU, so its cost is milliseconds per frame on the cell computer, measured next to the geometric method.

What the learned model did, and the mistake it exposed. The U-Net learned the training frames almost perfectly: 0.9998 IoU on the 10 legs held back for checking. On the 20 test legs it scored 0.968, with the centre about 3 mm off on nearly every frame, which is worse than geometry. An error that is the same size and direction on every frame is rarely the model. When we shrank the training outlines to the network's 320 × 200, we took the top-left pixel of every 4 × 4 block instead of its middle, so every label sat 1.5 px up and to the left of the leg, about 3 mm on the belt. The model learned exactly what we showed it. We checked the shift on a test shape (1.5 px before the fix, 0.04 px after), added a test so it cannot come back, and retrained both models on corrected labels.

What we decide. We wrote the rule down before training: the learned model stays only if it beats geometry by 0.01 IoU or 1 mm on the centre, with no extra refusals. The first training did not, because of the label mistake. Retrained on corrected labels, it still loses on the idealised frames, where geometry is exact, but with the camera's edge effects modelled it scores 0.984 against geometry's 0.967 with no extra refusals, so it passes. Keeping only the pixels both call leg does better again: 0.985, with the centre within 0.3 mm. So the cell uses the learned segmenter checked by the geometric one, which keeps running every frame. Training on edge-effect frames made no difference, 0.984 either way: the model did not need to see the camera's flaws to cope with them. The edge effects are a model with assumed numbers, so the real camera decides this again.

What each costs to run. On this computer's CPU, geometry took 93 to 105 ms per frame and the U-Net about 130 ms, measured while other training jobs shared the processor. Running both costs the sum, about 230 ms, against the 33 ms a 30 frames-per-second camera allows. Now that we know we keep both, making them fast is the next piece of work on this step.

11.3Segmenting real plant footage

Simulation only tells us so much, so we also ran segmentation on the real footage from the leg station: pale legs on a white modular belt, gloved hands, steel walls, legs touching. These clips come from a phone, so there is colour but no depth, and the depth method cannot run on them.

The deterministic method. A colour rule. We sampled real pixels on two frames and found that meat, skin, fat and cut faces alike, sits in a narrow band of redness and yellowness that the white belt, its shadows, the steel and the blue gloves do not. Pixels in that band are meat; each connected patch is one piece. It runs in milliseconds.

The learned method. Segment Anything (Kirillov et al. 2023), a model trained on over a billion masks to outline every distinct object in a picture, without being told what the objects are. It proposes the regions; the same colour rule then decides which regions are meat. This is the composition we want in the cell: the learned part is good at where one object ends and the next begins, and the deterministic part makes the decision we can audit.

11.4Finding the centre of gravity: geometry first

Once the leg is outlined, the cell needs its centre of gravity: the turn happens about it, and the grip has to hold against the leg's weight. The middle of the outline is not that point, because a leg's outline runs a long way down the thin hock and trotter, which weigh little.

How the geometric estimate works. The camera sees only the top of the leg. So we treat every leg pixel as a solid column of meat from the belt up to the surface the camera sees, work out each column's volume from its height and the patch of belt that pixel covers, and take the centre of all that volume. It assumes the meat is equally dense and that nothing under the surface is hollow. We compared it with the simplest answer, the centre of the outline, on the same 20 legs and 9 positions as segmentation, against the centre of mass the simulator computes from the leg's shape. The rule and the numbers we expected were written down first (experiments/2026-09-15-centre-of-gravity.md).

This is a simulated leg of uniform density; a real ham's bone and fat shift its centre of mass, which only weighed legs from the line can tell us.

11.5Intercepting and grasping the leg: what goes in, what comes out

Now we use everything we have measured so far to take hold of the leg while it moves. The outline, the centre of gravity and the shape of the leg along its length, together with how fast the belt is going and whether it is speeding up or slowing down, go into a grasp policy. The policy says where on the leg to grip and how, and nothing about which robot does it. Then each arm works out for itself how to get there on time. That is what lets the same grasp skill run on the SCARA and the six-axis arm, and on the next robot too, and it is how we find out whether the tool needs to tilt.

What goes in. One message per leg, LegPerception: when the frame was taken and how far the belt had moved; the centre of gravity; the outline centre; the leg's direction from ham to trotter, found as the direction from the centre of gravity toward the outline centre, which always sits 40 mm toward the thin end (11.4); its length and volume; and 40 cross sections along it, each with its middle, width and height. Beside it, BeltState: travel, speed and acceleration fitted to the last 0.3 s of encoder readings.

What comes out. GraspAction: the point between the closed jaws, on the leg, in belt coordinates, so it rides with the belt; the line the jaws close along; how far the tool leans; how wide to open; how long to close. No joint angles and no robot name.

The deterministic policy. Grip the shank two thirds of the way from the ham, at the middle of that cross section, jaws square to the leg there, opening the shank's width plus 40 mm. On a simulated leg at two angles, working only from the camera, it lands within 20 mm of the true shank point and within 8 degrees of square to it. The old grasp skills read the simulator's true pose, so this is the first grasp the cell can decide from what it sees.

The learned policy. It reads the same message and writes the same action. It does not start from nothing: it takes the deterministic grasp and learns only how far to move it along and across the leg, how far to turn the jaws, and whether to tilt the tool 0, 15 or 30 degrees. With nothing learned it is exactly the deterministic grasp. It is trained on 100 simulated legs the test never uses, each labelled with the best of a set of grasps tried on a floating gripper, and it runs as plain matrix maths so it fits inside a ROS 2 node without a GPU or torch.

The intercept. From the grasp point and the belt state, the planner finds the moment the arm can arrive exactly where that point will be, using the belt's acceleration so a belt that is speeding up or slowing down does not leave the arm reaching for where the leg used to be (10.1); it waits if the leg is not in reach yet, and says why when the leg would leave before the jaws close. The arm then comes down along its own axis onto the moving point and follows it while the jaws close.

ROS 2 and MoveIt. The messages are a ROS 2 package, and three nodes carry the chain: belt state, grasp policy (picked by a parameter) and one executor per arm, which asks for the joint angles at each waypoint and sends one timed trajectory to the arm's controller. MoveIt is now installed on this computer, and the arm descriptions it uses are generated from the simulator's own numbers: they match the simulated arms within 0.1 mm, and MoveIt's answers for the UR20 put the tool exactly where asked, tilted or not. A SCARA's tool can only point down, so its joint angles come from a short closed-form solver that answers the same request MoveIt does; the executor cannot tell the difference. Adding a robot means adding its description and config; the policy, messages and planner stay as they are.

First runs, end to end. The simulated cell now runs as ROS 2 nodes too: the camera's leg goes out as a message, the policy decides, the executor plans and solves, the simulated arm follows the trajectory, and the simulator scores the result against the truth. The first runs found five defects in the chain, all fixed; the worst was stray pixels at the leg's outline that made the leg look twice as long and put every grasp 155 mm off. 11.7 How a grasp is chosen, and how grasps are classified

Choosing. A grasp is a point on the leg, a line the jaws close along, a lean of the tool, an opening and a closing time, in belt coordinates (11.5). Two policies produce it from the same perceived leg. The rule grips the shank at 0.70 of the leg's length from the ham end, on the local centreline, jaws across the local direction, opening the widest part under the pads plus 40 mm, tool vertical; it moved from 0.66 to 0.70 today because at 0.66 the pads reached the waist. The learned policy starts from that grasp and predicts five things: how far to move it along the leg, how far across, how far to turn the jaw line, and which of three tool leans to use, 0°, 15° or 30°. Its input is the 126 numbers the perception message already carries: width, top height and centreline offset at 40 cross sections, plus length, volume, where the centre of gravity sits, the outline centre's offset from it, and the belt's speed and acceleration. With nothing learned its output is zero and it is the rule exactly, so a badly trained model degrades toward the rule and not toward anywhere else.

Classifying. Three classifications run on every grasp, and they are what a learned policy is trained and judged on. The tool lean is a three-way class chosen by the policy, and an arm that cannot lean refuses a leaned grasp before it moves. The fit is a rule decided before the arm moves: a two-jaw gripper needs 20 mm of spare opening each side of the widest shank under its pads; a centric three-finger gripper needs that much spare in half its opening, because its off-axis fingers sit half the open radius from the tool axis and land on any shank wider than that, which is why the three-finger gripper takes the leg end-on at the trotter and never across the shank. The outcome is the contract's verdict after the arm has moved: success, or one of unreachable, closed on nothing, slipped, lift not achieved. Success means the jaws stopped between half the shank's width and the widest part under the pads, and a 5 mm proof lift raised the tool at least 0.5 mm with the leg lagging it by no more than 2 mm. That 2 mm is measured: the three-finger hold on a trotter settles 1.6 to 1.8 mm whatever the lift height, which is contact settling, not slip, while the jaw on a shank lags under 0.1 mm.

Feedback that a grasp succeeded, on the real cell. The proof lift is the simulator's version of three signals a real cell has: the jaw position after closing, which says whether something the size of a shank is between the pads; the change in wrist load on the lift, which says the leg came with the tool; and a camera frame after the lift, which says where the leg now is. The third matters most, because a grasp can move the leg: a step close moved it 25 mm along its axis, a ramped close 2.4 mm median and 11 mm worst (11.6), and either way grasp success and the post-grasp pose are one measurement that the turn has to be planned on. The learned policy's labels come from the same three signals: every executed grasp, in simulation now and on the line later, is scored by them and becomes a training row without anyone labelling it. The pre-registered A/B in Table 11.5 is the gate: the learned policy replaces the rule only if it holds more legs through the lift, paired by leg, without missing the meeting point by more than 2 mm more.

Section 13

How the cell learns, and how it is taught

Three parts of the cell are trained models today: the one that outlines the leg, the one that finds its centre of gravity, and the one that chooses where to grip. All three were trained on simulated legs, where the right answer is known exactly. In the plant the right answer is not known, but the cell can score itself: the gripper reports whether it closed on meat, and the saw reports where it cut. This section says which of those scores can teach which skill, where trial-and-error learning is worth its cost, how a new skill is taught, and what a large pretrained robot model would and would not give us.

Every learned part of the cell is supervised, with labels from the simulator. That is the right choice while the simulator can execute every candidate answer and read the truth. It stops being available the day the cell runs on real legs, so the second question is which signals the plant produces for free. The third is where reinforcement learning earns a place. The fourth is the interface for teaching. The fifth is a vision-language-action model as a base. Sources are the repository's own code and experiment records, and the library's notes on residual learning, sim-to-real, policy composition and VLAs.

How these become training data. Every skill run already produces the triple the trainers need: the contract's inputs (the LegPerception message, the belt state), the action taken (the GraspAction, the orient chunk) and the outcome with its numbers. A scorer trained this way is also a monitor: a proposed grasp it scores low on is refused before the arm moves, which is a precondition the contract can carry.

13.3Reinforcement learning: the orient manoeuvre, and nowhere else yet

The grasp point is a one-shot decision, and the simulator can execute every candidate and hand back the best. Exploration buys nothing there; supervised offsets on a 126-feature vector are the cheaper and more testable answer. Perception has exact labels. Real-robot RL costs product on every step. That leaves one place: the turn on the belt.

The orient skill is a sequence of 8-step chunks at 10 Hz under contact, with belt friction from 0.15 to 0.5, a trotter that drapes, and 20 legs that differ in mass and length. The scripted turn about the centre of gravity is the base; a bounded residual on top of it is the candidate. This is the design the library recommends for the cell (library/topics/learning-grasp-lift-and-orient-skills.md, stage 2), with the reward equal to the contract's success check, 1 when every check passes and 0 otherwise, so the reward and the evaluator cannot disagree.

13.4Teaching a new skill: the interface

A skill is a contract plus an implementation (src/robotics/core/skill_library/contract.py: inputs, preconditions, outputs, success checks, failure modes; recovery on the graph). The implementation can be a rule, a planner or a model. A learned skill is the same contract plus a checkpoint, whose SHA-256 is one of its inputs and whose match is one of its preconditions (Table 6.2). The task graph (graph.py) routes by outcome and does not know which kind it is running. That is the property the teaching interface is built on: teaching changes the implementation and never the contract, so the graph, the tests and the runner's checks are unchanged by it.

Four pieces, each grounded in something that exists, sized for the next two weeks.

  1. Demonstrations recorded through the contract. A demonstration is a run of the skill with a different decider: the scripted expert that reads the true pose (section 6), a candidate grid (the grasp labels), a person on a SpaceMouse later. run_skill already type-checks the inputs, runs the preconditions, re-checks the claimed success and returns the outcome with every check's number. A recorder around it writes one row per run: the contract's inputs, the action, the SkillResult. The floating-gripper expert for orient and the candidate grid for grasp are the first two demonstrators; both exist in the simulator.
  2. One dataset record per skill. The convention is set in CLAUDE.md: a DATASET.md next to every dataset with source, robot, date, operator, episode count and known issues. None exists yet: the two trainings so far cached rendered frames under outputs/ without one. The first three records are the segmentation frames, the centre-of-gravity frames and the grasp labels, each naming the population seed, the noise seed and the camera model, so a checkpoint's name (<model>-<dataset>-<git-sha>-<step>.pt) resolves to a record.
  3. Training scripts under scripts/train/ that export what the node can load. centre_correction.py and leg_segmenter.py exist. The grasp policy's script is next, and its export is the pattern: weights written to .npz, loaded by numpy in the ROS 2 node, because that Python has no torch. The centre-of-gravity and segmentation models are torch modules today and need the same export before they can run in the node; until then they run in the simulator's environment only.
  4. The pre-registered gate before a model replaces a rule. The rule in experiments/2026-09-15-intercept-grasp.md is the template: the same 20 test legs × 9 arrivals at 0.30 m/s, with 0.20 and 0.40 m/s held out of training; the learned policy replaces the rule only if it holds more legs through the 5 mm lift, paired by leg and arrival (McNemar, p < 0.05), with its median meeting miss not worse by more than 2 mm; otherwise the rule stays and the model is reported. Every new skill gets the same four lines before its first training run: the test set, the paired test, the non-inferiority margin, and the failure modes the rule never produces that the model must not introduce.

What a team could build in two weeks: the recorder (a wrapper on run_skill and a parquet file per skill), the three DATASET.md records, the grasp training script with its .npz export and the A/B run under the rule above. The orient residual's stage 2 record is written in that window and run after it. Anything on a real leg waits for the plant's cut tolerance, which every pass bar in this plan assumes.

The order. A parameter moves up one level only when the level below has shown it should. Level one is the hand-set value with its reason, which is where every parameter is now. Level two is a sweep over the 20 test legs through the same runner and the same judge as Table 11.7; it costs minutes and it says whether one value serves every leg. Level three is a learned output, where the sweep shows the best value differs from leg to leg and the perception carries enough to predict it: the grasp station and lean are there already, in the policy of 11.7. Level four is the residual policy of 13.3, for the parameters that interact through contact and cannot be swept one at a time: the pivot point, the lift and the turn rate together. A parameter that reaches level three or four keeps its level-one value as the fallback the contract promises, so nothing the cell learns can move it outside what the sweep already showed safe.

13.7Sub-goals and hierarchy: how a whole plant runs as one graph

A primitive in this codebase is a skill with a contract (src/robotics/core/skill_library/contract.py). Its preconditions are the states it may start from. Its success checks are its goal condition. Its failure modes are the outcomes the graph routes on, and graph.py refuses to build a task with an outcome that has no edge. In the language of hierarchical reinforcement learning that is an option: an initiation set, a policy and a termination condition. The library holds no note on Sutton, Precup and Singh 1999, where the option is defined, so that correspondence is stated from memory (unverified). The three parts are the contract's whatever they are called, and the runner enforces them: it re-checks a claimed success and reports success_not_verified when the checks disagree (3.1).

Six primitives exist as contracts with code under src/applications/pork_leg_alignment/skills/: perceive (EstimateLegFromCamera, leg_estimate.py), select grasp (SelectShankGrasp, SelectTrotterEndGrasp), acquire shank (AcquireShank), acquire trotter end-on (AcquireTrotterEnd), rotate on belt (RotateOnBelt) and pick and place (PickAndPlace, both in rotate_on_belt.py). The first four have unit tests under tests/applications/pork_leg_alignment/skills/, ten test functions in three files. The two in rotate_on_belt.py have no unit test file; their evidence is the 80 approach-B and 40 approach-A episodes of Table 11.7, run through scripts/measure/alignment_approaches.py. Four primitives that 8.1 counts are not built as contracts. Push has no code (Table 14.1). Inspect exists only as the simulator's judge, sim/saw.py; no skill declares it, and the verification camera before the ramp is not built (Table 10.1). Transport is a step inside PickAndPlace, not a skill with the slipped_in_transport outcome the library's design gives it (library/topics/learning-grasp-lift-and-orient-skills.md, section 4). Place is the lower, open, retreat and clear steps inside RotateOnBelt, with their own failures released_late and dropped, and not a skill of its own.

The levels above are the same shape. A composed skill is a graph of primitives with a goal condition of its own: align a leg for the saw ends when the blade cuts within 10 mm of the hock at entry and exit and within 5° of square (Table 11.7; both tolerances are assumed until the plant gives its own). Inside it, each primitive's goal condition is a sub-goal: a held shank, a hock on the blade plane, jaws open before x = 0.48 m. A job is a composed skill run once per product with the job's terminals, aligned, let_pass to the manual station and stop_cell (Figure 4). A plant is jobs sharing primitives: the leg cell and the loin puller infeed share all nine of the primitives 8.1 counts (pork-plant-skill-tree.md, ranking row 7). Each level's goal condition is a sub-goal for the level above, and each level judges the one below by its outcome without looking inside it: the runner re-checks a primitive, the saw and not the orient skill decides the composed skill, the shift's tally decides the job.

The two worked jobs. In the leg cell the job passes "cut inside tolerance" down to the composed skill, which passes "the shank at 0.70 of the length, jaws square" to select grasp and "hock on the blade plane, heading −90°" to rotate on belt. What comes back is one outcome per primitive: from the grasp, success, unreachable, closed_on_nothing, slipped or lift_not_achieved; from the turn, success, unreachable, slipped, misaligned, released_late or dropped; from any of them, the runner's precondition_failed and success_not_verified. On the loin puller infeed (section 14) the composed graph is the same and the sub-goals change: the grasp station becomes the section at the centre of gravity (14.2), the turn's target becomes the bone edge on the fixture's datum (14.3, unverified), and the composed skill's goal condition becomes the fixture crossing score of sim/infeed_fixture.py in place of the saw (14.4). Approach A is closed to a 20 kg middle (14.2), so that job never calls pick and place. No primitive's contract changes between the two jobs. What changes is the sub-goal passed to it and the judge above it.

Where learning sits in this hierarchy today: nowhere that decides. Every primitive's policy is a rule (the shank rule, the scripted turn about the centre of gravity) or a supervised model on top of a rule (the segmenter, the centre-of-gravity correction and the grasp offsets of Table 13.1). The routing is fixed: each declared outcome has exactly one edge, written by hand, and the graph raises ContractError at construction if one is missing. Hierarchical reinforcement learning would put learning at three places. (a) The routing: a high-level policy that picks the next primitive from the state, the policy over options. (b) The sub-goals: what the composed skill passes down, where to grip, how high to carry, where to release. (c) The primitive's own low-level policy, which for this cell is the bounded residual of 13.3.

The results on this page pick (b), and they pick supervised learning for it before any reinforcement learning. The largest loss is after release: every cut-angle failure above 5° in Table 11.7 came from the hold-down's lead-in turning a leg the arm had set down within 1.3 mm median (UR20) or 2.3 mm (SR-20iA) of the blade plane (11.6). No level of the arm's hierarchy controls that; it is a fixture model to fix. The second loss is a sub-goal error: closing the jaws moves the leg 20 to 25 mm toward its thin end, so a turn planned on the pre-grasp pose set the hock 22 to 24 mm outboard and scored 0 of 3 from the camera, and 2 of 3 with a second look after the grasp (11.6). The fix is a sub-goal recomputed after the grasp, which the graph already has an edge for, and a grasp point that moves the leg less, which is the learned offset of 11.7 with labels the simulator hands over for every candidate. Both are supervised, with a pre-registered gate (13.4). The routing has little to learn: the alignment graph has three terminals and one sensible edge per outcome, and its only open choice, retry against let_pass, is set by the takt, 2 to 4 s per lane at 1,000 head per hour (derived, 8.1). The library also records why a learned routing is not free: on the same events, "options and hierarchical RL ... can converge to suboptimal policies because the hierarchy prunes the policy space", shown on a Minecraft-like world of 10 tasks on 10 maps with 3 trials each (Toro Icarte et al. 2018, Fig. 3, as skill-composition-action-outcome-input.md reads it). That note calls a reward machine the formal counterpart of this task graph; the graph is one written by hand. (c) comes after the hold-down is fixed, when the two slips in 20 on the SR-20iA's turn (Table 11.7) are the largest loss left: the residual of 13.3, reward equal to the contract's success check, about 2.1 hours of one process per 160,000 policy steps on this simulator.

What the library holds on hierarchical methods, and what it does not. It has no note on HIRO, HAC, feudal networks, the option-critic or skill chaining as Konidaris defined it; no claim is made about them here. What it does hold. A high-level policy over latent skills: SPiRL picks a latent every H steps under a learned prior (Pertsch, Lee and Lim 2020), and OPAL's ablation puts a number on temporal abstraction, 55.3 ± 3.8 with one-step primitives against 81.1 with ten-step, antmaze in simulation, 4 seeds (Ajay et al. 2021, Table 5). A sub-goal-passing hierarchy across bodies: a high level that outputs an x-y sub-goal delta and a per-morphology low level, simulation only (Hejna et al. 2020). A meta-controller over a growing library that picks a skill index and a sub-goal embedding every step: LOTUS, forward transfer 74.0 ± 3.0 against 56 for experience replay on LIBERO-Object, 3 seeds, 20 trials per task (Wan et al. 2024). A VLA that writes its own sub-goal in text: π0.5, where the high-level step helped even when skipped at inference (Fig. 13, and Table 13.5). Chaining: a policy-sequencing baseline failed the fourth furniture sub-task because the initial state distribution had shifted, and pulling each skill's terminal states toward the next skill's initiation set took full-task success from 0 to 56 percent on one model (Lee et al. 2021); widening a stationary skill's start states dropped Pick from 95 to 45 percent and the chain to 37.7 (Gu et al. 2022). That is the failure the contract's preconditions exist to catch, and the reason the library's plan evaluates each skill on its predecessor's recorded terminal states before composing (learning-grasp-lift-and-orient-skills.md, section 5). Behaviour trees: the only note is Nav2's BT Navigator, where recovery logic changes by editing the tree's XML and not the code (Nav2 docs); the task graph does the same with its edge table. Older than all of these, Morrow and Khosla define a skill as a composition of sensorimotor primitives that solves a recurring sub-goal, each primitive a controller, a trajectory and event detectors (1997), which is the contract's policy plus its checks.

The dependency rule in ARCHITECTURE.md is what makes this list checkable: nothing under src/robotics/ imports anything under src/applications/, and tests/test_layering.py fails the build if that changes. Every file in this subsection is used by the loin cell as it stands.

14.2What is re-parameterised, not rewritten

The product model. sim/product.py is a leg: a nine-station profile (LEG_PROFILE), a hock at 0.76 of the length, a trotter welded on, and a population drawn from a mass range of 9.0 to 15.5 kg and a length range of 655 to 815 mm. A loin needs its own LoinConfig in the same shape: a profile read off the piece, a mass range, the flat underside that stops a piece rolling, and a population from one seed. It has no second body and no weld, because nothing is cut off it in this cell. What goes in the profile is unmeasured. The only public mass figure in the library is the USDA cutout: the loin primal is 25.17 percent of a 97.5 kg carcass and the belly 16.28 percent (pork-processing-line.md 4.1). Split evenly between the two sides that is 12.3 kg of bone-in loin, or 20.2 kg if the puller takes the whole middle (derived from the cutout table; the piece at Prestage is unmeasured). That difference decides the arm before any run: a 20 kg middle is at the rated payload of the 20 kg-class arms in section 7, so approach A, which carries the piece, is closed to it, and approach B, which turns it on the belt, is the only approach left open.

The cell layout. sim/scene.py's CellConfig already carries belt speed, arm, arm mount, gripper, depth cameras and camera yaw as fields; saw and hold_down are optional and are left None. The loin cell adds one field, the infeed fixture of 14.4, in the saw's place. The mounts in DEFAULT_MOUNTS were chosen on 2026-09-14 to keep the open edge clear for overhanging trotters; which side of the belt the loin puller's datum is on is unknown (unverified), so the mount is chosen again from the same reach test (section 12: the UR20 reached all 120 grid points at three heights, the SR-20iA 101 of 120 at 50 and 180 mm and none at 350 mm). The belt speed at the station is unmeasured; the runs use 0.30 m/s as the leg's do and hold 0.20 and 0.40 m/s out, as Table 11.5 does.

The camera mount. robotics/hardware/cameras.py hangs its cameras on overhead_cam_mount, a body in this cell's cell.xml (ARCHITECTURE.md, known gaps). The loin cell's XML has to define that body over its own pick zone, and the field lesson applies: the Gemini 335L's field must hold the longest piece at any arrival, which on legs it did not for the longest of the three camera-in-the-loop runs.

The perception's product description. LegEstimate (skills/leg_estimate.py) is a centreline of K points from one end to the other, a width at each, a heading, a centre of gravity, and the encoder reading they are valid at. LegPerception (grasping/leg_perception.py, carried as meat_cell_msgs/LegPerception) is the same with one top height per section. Three of the four parts fit a loin as they are: a piece lying on the belt has a centreline, widths along it and a centre of gravity, and perceive_leg.py's cross-section binning computes them from any mask. The heading does not fit. On a leg the axis is signed by the centre of gravity sitting 40 mm from the outline centre toward the thin end (11.4, perceive_leg.py). Whether a loin's outline is uneven enough end to end for that rule is unverified, and a design that leans on it would be a guess. The safe description adds one field: which side of the piece the bone lies on, an asymmetry across the piece rather than along it, read from the height profile across each section. LegPerception carries one top height per section and cannot hold that, so the message gets the field and, under the ROS 2 rule in CLAUDE.md, a new message type rather than a changed one. This is the one place the transfer changes an interface, and it is the "perception configuration" that the caption of Figure 8.4 says every covered job still needs.

The grasp rule's station. The shank rule grips at 0.70 of the length from the ham (SHANK_GRASP_FRACTION in skills/shank_grasp.py, SHANK_FRACTION in grasping/shank_grasp_rule.py), because the shank is bone with a 78 to 110 mm width and the jaw's 120 mm pads clear the waist there by 37 mm. That is a landmark on a leg. The library can source no equivalent rigid handle on a loin at any fraction of its length (unverified). The starting rule for the loin is the section nearest the centre of gravity, jaws across the piece: it puts the pivot of approach B's turn under the jaws, so the turn moves no mass sideways. The jaw-fit precondition in AcquireShank already refuses a grasp wider than the opening before the arm moves, and it applies to that section as it did to the shank. Whether the rule holds a loin is the first thing the runs measure, not something this section knows.

14.3What has to be re-learned or re-measured

Segmentation. leg_segmentation.py's method is product-free: height above an image of the empty belt, a belt region tested in world coordinates, a 300 mm height cap so the arm is not taken for product. Its name is the only leg thing about it. Its two thresholds are legs': the 25 mm minimum standing height and the 150 mm of overhang past the open edge for the trotter. Both are re-measured on the loin population, and the learned segmenter (learned_leg_segmentation.py, trained by scripts/train/leg_segmenter.py) is retrained on frames rendered from the new model and measured on the same error table as 11.2.

Centre of gravity. The column centroid (robotics/perception/centre_of_gravity.py) is geometry and carries. The learned corrector on top of it (learned_centre_of_gravity.py, trained by scripts/train/centre_correction.py) was trained on legs and sizes its grid, 96 by 96 cells of 10 mm, and its height scale for them (ARCHITECTURE.md, known gaps). It is retrained, and the grid is resized if a middle is longer than 0.96 m (its length is unverified).

The grasp offsets. The learned grasp policy (grasping/learned_grasp_policy.py) learns an offset along and across the piece, a jaw turn and a tilt on top of the deterministic rule, from 100 simulated pieces labelled with the best of a set of floating-gripper grasps (11.5). Its starting rule changes (14.2), so its labels and training are redone on the loin population, and the A/B of Table 11.5 is run again under the same rule: learned replaces deterministic only if it holds more pieces through the 5 mm proof lift, paired by piece.

The orient manoeuvre's target. RotateOnBelt (skills/rotate_on_belt.py) turns the piece until its heading equals TARGET_HEADING_RAD and its hock lies on the saw's blade plane, keeping the centre of gravity where it is along the belt: the target is a point on the piece and a plane in the cell. For the loin puller both halves are unknown. The skill-tree note writes the job as intercept_and_grasp, orient_on_belt and place_into_fixture and says nothing about the pose the machine needs; which edge must lie against what datum, how square, and with what tolerance are in no source the library holds (unverified). The runs use a working assumption and say so: piece square to belt travel, bone side toward a datum line, within the leg's assumed 10 mm and 5 degrees. The manoeuvre itself, the tool held at a fixed pose in the piece's frame with belt travel added every tick, is unchanged. Only the two numbers that define "aligned" change, and they are the customer's or the vendor's to give.

14.4Showing the transfer in simulation

The leg cell's evidence today came from one protocol: a record written before the runs, 20 legs from one seed, the same arrivals for every condition, one runner, the saw as the judge, both arms. The transfer is shown the same way, and what it demonstrates is how few files it touches.

  • Record. experiments/<date>-loin-infeed-transfer.md from experiments/TEMPLATE.md, hypothesis fixed first: the leg cell's skill graph, re-parameterised as in 14.2 and with no change to any file under src/robotics/, aligns at least as many of 20 loins as it did legs (14 of 20 on the SR-20iA, 16 of 20 on the UR20 at 15 degrees of tilt, Results table of the record) against the assumed tolerance. The counter-hypothesis is live: a piece with no rigid handle slips in the jaws during the turn, which the leg did once in 20.
  • Products. loin_population(20, seed=0) from the new model, drawn as random_leg draws legs, from one size variable with scatter on top, so a heavy piece is also a long one.
  • Runner. scripts/measure/alignment_approaches.py. Its episode loop, the Episode row, judge() and summarise() are product-free. Its imports are not: leg_population, HOCK_FRACTION, SawConfig and HoldDownConfig are the leg cell's. It gets a --product flag that selects the population, the estimate skills and the judge, and nothing else in it changes. That is the one file outside the application package that the transfer edits.
  • Judge. The leg has sim/saw.py, which scores where the blade plane crosses the piece at first contact and at exit. The loin cell has no blade, so it gets sim/infeed_fixture.py: a plane across the belt at the fixture's x, where the piece's pose is read as it crosses and scored as the bone edge's offset from the datum and its heading error, against the same 10 mm and 5 degrees until the vendor or the plant gives real numbers (unverified). The same shape of result as CutResult, with no cut process and no hold-down.
  • Arms. UR20 at 0, 15 and 30 degrees of tilt and the SR-20iA at 0, jaw on both, 20 pieces each: 80 episodes from ground truth, then the same with --pose-source camera, as the leg's ran.

Effort, one engineer, simulation only: eight working days. Product model, two days: the leg model needed three shape fixes in one day (a rolling ellipsoid, a hull that bridged the waist, a sagging trotter weld; product.py), and a loin has one body and no weld, so one day for the model and one for the population, its variants figure and its tests. Layout and judge, one day: the CellConfig fields exist, the fixture judge is saw.py's crossing score without the cut process, and the layout follows from the timed cycle. Perception, three days: one for the bone-side field, its message and the heading rule; one to render frames and retrain the segmenter and the centre-of-gravity corrector; one for the error tables on the 20 pieces. Runs, two days: compute is not the cost, since the leg's 80 episodes ran at a median of 1.5 to 1.8 s each (wall_s column, experiments/data/2026-09-15-alignment-approaches/), but the leg's shakedown on legs 0 to 2 produced eight amendments before any condition ran, so one day is the shakedown and one is the 80 episodes, the camera runs and the record. The estimate assumes the piece's dimensions, mass range and required pose arrive from the plant. Without them the eight days show that the software transfers to an invented loin, which is the software claim and not the plant claim.
