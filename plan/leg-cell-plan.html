Aligning pork legs to the trotter saw, and the skill library it starts

A leg arrives on the belt at any yaw. The cell turns it so the hock rides the saw's blade plane and the trotter hangs off the open edge. Each step is a skill with a contract. The same skills, and the compositions between them, are how the rest of the plant gets automated.

What this plan proposes
  1. Cell: overhead depth camera, one arm behind the far rail, a hold-down belt at the saw. The arm turns the leg on the belt without lifting it, unless the experiment in 7.1 shows lifting works better.
  2. Task: perceive the leg, choose where to intercept it, grasp it, orient it so the hock sits on the blade plane, release before the hold-down, verify. Every outcome of every skill is routed to a next skill or a terminal; the graph refuses to build otherwise.
  3. Deterministic first: depth keypoints, IK and contract checks are classical. Learning goes where the tests fail: the orient manoeuvre under leg-to-leg variation, and perception if keypoints miss their gate.
  4. Composition: skills are distributions, and there are three ways to compose them. Combining actions promises something about each step; combining specifications promises the outcome, exactly only under deterministic dynamics; fusing inputs promises only the belief. Each skill in the cell is assigned one, and its contract adds the detector for what that type cannot promise (4.3).
  5. Wednesday demo: one learned orient skill, run unchanged on a SCARA and a six-axis arm, acting in the belt frame through each arm's own IK. The SCARA run exceeds the SR-20iA's published wrist inertia and is labelled as such; the manoeuvre choice is Lakshya's (6.2).
  6. Plant: the same primitive skills (perceive, track, intercept, grasp, turn, place, hold, cut, verify) compose into the other manual jobs on the line; section 8 ranks which cell comes next by skills reused.

Section 1

The task and the cell

Legs come down a conveyor belt at random angles. Before they reach a saw at the end of the belt, each leg has to be turned so its foot sticks out over the edge, with the hock joint exactly where the blade will cut. We built a computer simulation of the whole station, with the belt, the saw and a robot arm, so we can try ideas safely and measure how well each one works before touching real hardware.

Neil set alignment as the first task, starting with the simpler version of the problem. At the leg station today, workers turn whole bone-in legs one-handed so the trotter reaches the open right-hand edge of the belt. Legs arrive touching, at anything from square to about 45 degrees off. Further down the belt, a circular saw cuts the trotter off at the hock. The cell goes into Prestage Foods of Iowa near Eagle Grove.

What aligned means, in numbers the saw can judge. The sim does not score alignment by eye. The saw scores it: when the blade surface meets the leg surface the cut starts, the blade pushes back with a feed resistance, and when the leg has travelled its width the cut completes and reports the offset from the hock at entry and exit and the angle off square. The pre-registered pass is within ±10 mm and ±5 degrees, both † assumed, since no tolerance has come from the customer.

Several of these checks exposed mistakes in our model, and we fixed each one before moving on.

Section 3

Skills with contracts, tasks as graphs

We break the job into small robot skills: find the leg, grab it, turn it, let go. Every skill states up front what it needs, what it promises to deliver, and every way it can fail. Skills are chained into a task, and for each possible failure the task says what happens next, so the robot never ends up without a plan.

We are building an architecture where a skill can be deterministic motion, classical control, optimisation, a learned policy or a perception module, and every skill carries the same contract: inputs, preconditions, outputs, success conditions, failure conditions and recovery. Tasks are graphs of skills, so a new application reuses most of the graph and adds only what is new about it. The library lives in src/robotics/core/skill_library/ and imports nothing from the simulator or about pork. Recovery belongs to the task graph, because what to do after a failure depends on the task.

3.1One contract, as it runs today

The runner does not trust the skill. Inputs are type-checked; a failing precondition stops the skill before it moves; a skill may end only in success or a failure it declared; and a claimed success is re-checked, and reported as success_not_verified if the checks disagree. Every check returns the number it decided on, so a failed episode explains itself in values.

acquire_shank src/applications/pork_leg_alignment/skills/shank_grasp.py inputs shank_grasp : ShankGrasp tool target on the shank, world frame preconditions jaw_spare_m jaws open ≥ 40 mm wider than the shank outputs grip_opening_m : float measured opening after closing lift_proof : LiftProof tool and shank heights at closure success grip_opening_m between ½ shank width and widest shank under pads part_minus_tool_m shank rose with the tool, lag ≤ 1 mm failures unreachable no IK for any approach height, grasp or lift closed_on_nothing jaws closed past half the shank width slipped jaws closed, shank did not rise with the tool lift_not_achieved tool could not rise ≥ 0.5 mm under the load runner adds success · precondition_failed · success_not_verified

3.2Where learning goes in this graph

We use deterministic methods wherever they are reliable, and learning where uncertainty, soft tissue, contact or variation make it necessary. In this graph that puts learning in two places, each used only after the deterministic version fails its gate:

  • orient: turning a leg on a belt is a contact task with belt friction, leg-to-leg shape variation and a trotter that drapes. A scripted rotation about the centre of gravity is the baseline; a learned policy, or a learned residual on it, replaces it only if the scripted one misses the saw tolerance on the 20-leg population.
  • locate_leg: hock and trotter keypoints from depth geometry first. The old slab estimator used the area centroid and was off by 47 mm and 19 degrees on a leg, because a leg's centroid is in the ham while the task is defined at the hock. A learned keypoint model enters only if geometry misses its gate, especially on touching legs.

The contract does not change when the implementation does. A learned orient skill declares the same inputs, preconditions, success checks and failure modes as a scripted one, and the graph around it does not know the difference.

Section 4

Composing skills: action, outcome, input

Once single skills work, we want to combine them into new ones, the way a football player combines running and dribbling. There are three ways to combine skills: blend what they do, combine what they are trying to achieve, or combine what they see. Each way comes with a different kind of guarantee. Combining skills can also produce surprises, good or bad, so every combination gets tested.

A skill is a distribution in two senses: over actions given what it observes, and over the trajectories and outcomes it produces when it runs. Composing skills is an operation on one of those distributions, or on the inputs that feed them. Which one decides what the composed skill can promise. Source: library/topics/skill-composition-action-outcome-input.md.

4.1Same two skills, two compositions, different behaviour

A corridor of states 0 to 8, goal states 0, 3 and 4, start at 5. Skill A wants goal 0 or 3; skill B wants 0 or 4. The composed task is A AND B: reach 0. Finishing at a desired goal pays +2, at another goal −2, every step −0.1. Deterministic, so the exactness conditions hold.

Outcome composition has limits too. In van Niekerk's counterexample with stochastic outcomes, max-composition picks an action worth 0.9 where the true OR optimum is 1.0; the lower bound still holds, but neither operator's choice is justified once the assumptions go. A wet leg sliding on a belt is not deterministic, so for this cell outcome composition gives bounds, and the saw measures the rest.

Input composition has its own quiet failure. Fusing a depth estimate of the hock (102 ± 4 mm) with a laser line (96 ± 2 mm) and a prior (100 ± 20 mm) gives 97.2 ± 1.8 mm; drop the laser and it gives 101.9 ± 3.9 mm, handled by construction. Bias the laser by +10 mm and the fused estimate moves 8 mm while the reported uncertainty stays at 1.8 mm: the product carries no signal that its experts disagree † illustrative sensor numbers.

4.2Non-additivity and emergence

Definition used here: a composition is additive when the composite's outcome equals the operator applied to the constituents' outcomes, so AND reaches exactly what both accept. The exactness theorems are exactly the cases where additivity is proven. Non-additivity is any departure; emergence is a departure into an outcome neither constituent produces, useful or harmful. Doing X and Y together gives the union of their outcomes only inside those theorems' assumptions.

Section 5

Skills as distributions in a latent space

A learned skill can be stored as a point in a space of skills, where nearby points behave alike. That would let us mix and adjust skills without teaching every variation from scratch. The research shows this only works under specific conditions, so for now we use the idea inside individual skills and keep each skill's checks outside it.

A learned skill is a distribution over actions given what the robot observes. Embedding skills in a shared latent space is what would let the library interpolate between them, add them, plan over them and carry them to another robot: running and dribbling as two points whose combination is a third skill neither demonstrated. The evidence says this works under specific conditions, and that no published latent carries a precondition or a success test. Source for this section: library/topics/latent-skill-representations.md, about 55 papers read in full.

5.1Adding skills in latent space works only when trained for it

A point between two skills can decode to an action nobody demonstrated. A latent that lies between two trained skills can decode to actions that appear nowhere in the data. On a 1-D walk whose data actions lie only in [−1, −0.8] and [0.8, 1], a Gaussian skill prior sampled in between, and the policy built on it averaged −66 reward against −2.2 with a diffusion prior (Venkatraman et al. 2023, App. D). On a real Sawyer, the mean of two push-skill latents "was not reliable for half of the synthetic goals" (Julian et al. 2018).

Collapse is measured in robot skill models. A skill VAE on CALVIN fell from 28.3 to 16.2 percent five-in-a-row success when β went from 0.01 to 0.1, "an over-regularized model which learns to ignore the latent plans" (Mees et al. 2022, 3 seeds). None of SPiRL, SkiLD, OPAL, CompILE, EXTRACT or QueST reports a β ablation.

Latents learned from video encode the wrong motion. Distractors raised a latent-action probe error from 2.55 to 22.09 (Nikulin et al., 4 environments × 3 seeds). On a belt every background pixel moves. † That camera-frame codes would encode belt travel is a prediction, untested.

5.2The generative head decides what composition does

The same composition gives different skills depending on how each skill's distribution is represented. The figure uses the worked example in generative-models-vae-and-diffusion.md: skill 1 is a leg rotation demonstrated two ways, modes at −1 and +1 in normalised action units; expert 2 is a Gaussian preference, for instance "stay near the saw side". Both are illustrative, not fitted to data.

Section 6

One learned skill, two arms

Tomorrow's demonstration shows one taught skill, turning a leg on the belt, running unchanged on two very different robots: a SCARA arm, which can only move flat and turn, and a six-joint arm. The skill describes how the leg should move, and each robot works out how to move its own joints to follow that. This is what lets the same skill run on both.

The Wednesday proof of concept: one learned orient skill, one checkpoint, run unchanged on the FANUC SR-20iA SCARA and the UR20 doing the same manoeuvre. It is the smallest real instance of skills that work across platforms. What makes it honest is stating exactly what transferred, and measuring it against what the arms alone lose.

6.1What the literature has and has not shown

Most "cross-embodiment" results are co-training: one model copes with mixed data from robots it was trained on. CrossFormer, trained on 20 embodiments, says it does "not yet show significant positive transfer" (Doshi et al. 2024, Sec. 5), and π0 never tests a robot held out of pretraining (Black et al. 2024). Every real result on an arm left out of training expresses actions as end-effector poses and relies on that arm's own IK and controller. No paper found transfers a policy to a four-axis arm or a SCARA † one search pass; an absence, not a result.

6.2The design

The only grasp both arms can make is the shank grasp from above with the tool vertical; the trotter end-on grasp needs tilt and the SCARA refuses it. With the tool vertical, the manoeuvre lives in x, y and yaw at a fixed height: the SCARA's native space, and a subset of the UR20's. So the policy acts in the leg's world, not the arm's, and neither arm appears in its training data.

Open decision

The two research notes behind this section disagree on which manoeuvre the shared skill should be, and the disagreement comes down to the SCARA's wrist.

  • Grasp and rotate on the belt (cross-embodiment-skill-transfer.md). The shank grasp from above already works on both arms. But turning a gripped leg accelerates its inertia about J4 whether or not the belt carries its weight, and that is 1.06× the SR-20iA's limit through the centre of gravity and 4.06× on the shank.
  • Planar push, tool vertical (latent-skill-representations.md). The arm never carries the leg's inertia, so the SCARA stays inside its rating. But a point push does not repeat (3.4 to 11.7 mm spread in the MIT dataset), so it has to be a line contact against a datum, which the cell does not have yet.

Gripping through the centre of gravity is not available: it lies in the ham, about 250 mm wide on the default leg, and the jaw opens to 180 mm. The shank grasp is the one both arms share, and there the SCARA is several times over its rating. Recommendation: run Wednesday's demo as grasp-and-rotate, because it exists on both arms today, and label the SCARA run on screen as over its published J4 inertia and not deployable as shown. Build the line push against a datum as the SCARA's production candidate after Wednesday. Whether FANUC permits any inertia overshoot at reduced acceleration is † unverified, a question for FANUC.

Warning

The SCARA can succeed in simulation at a move the real arm cannot make. The default leg is 1.06× the SR-20iA's J4 inertia limit gripped through its centre of gravity and 4.06× gripped on the shank 350 mm away, and neither simulated arm has torque limits. That number goes on screen next to the SCARA run.

Other known risks: the leg slides instead of turning (friction is randomised for this reason); the arms sag under load differently (absolute pose targets, replanned at 10 Hz, absorb a steady lag, and Mirage's delta-action collapse is the evidence for absolute); MuJoCo's flat jaw holds 0.60 to 0.70 of its rating on a cylinder, so a slip may be the contact model. rll has no torch and the GPU driver is mismatched until a reboot, so training runs on CPU; its time is unmeasured.

Section 7

Two decisions: the arm and the perception

Two early choices shape the station. The first is which type of robot arm to use, compared on how well it survives washdown, how fast and costly it is, and what it can physically do for this task. The second is whether we need a trained vision model to find the leg, or whether measuring its shape with a depth camera is enough.

Two choices shape the cell: a SCARA or a six-axis arm, and learned or deterministic perception. For the arm we compare the two on reliability, speed, cost, what each gains or loses on this task, and how quickly the hardware moves to another task or customer. Each row gives the evidence we have and what still has to be measured or quoted.

Why the orient skill grips the leg. Pushing at a single point does not repeat on any arm. A wet belt and a soft leg will not do better. So the orient skill either holds the leg while turning it, or pushes a flat contact against a fixed stop.

What settles it. The pre-registered experiment (experiments/2026-09-15-alignment-approaches.md): 8 conditions, 20 legs each, 160 episodes, each judged by the saw. Grasp-and-rotate against pick-and-place, both arms, both grippers, tool tilt 0, 15 and 30 degrees on the UR20. The rule written before any run: if grasp-and-rotate wins and tilt gains no more than 2 legs of 20, the SCARA stays in on cost and speed; if carrying or tilt is needed, the plan proposes the six-axis arm. It is blocked on one model fix, the arm servo sag in 2.3.

7.2Learned or deterministic perception

The quantity the saw needs is anatomical: where the hock is. Estimating the leg's centre and main axis misses it: the old slab estimator was off by 47 mm and 19 degrees on a leg, because a leg's area centroid sits in the ham. So perception outputs keypoints (hock, trotter tip, ham butt) and yaw, with depth required rather than preferred: on a 30 mm thick rigid slab a monocular estimator already spent 2.0 mm mean and 6.5 mm worst of a 2 mm budget (experiments/2026-09-08-perception-ingestion.md).

Section 8

From one cell to the plant

The leg station is the first of many jobs in the plant that people do by hand today. Most of those jobs reuse the same basic skills: seeing a piece, grabbing it, moving it, placing it. By counting which skills each job needs, we can choose next the stations that reuse the most of what we have already built.

Every manual job on a pork line is a skill or a composition of skills. The plan for the plant is to write each job as a graph over a shared library and count what it reuses. Three sourced facts set what automating a job is worth:

  • Pieces per minute drive injury risk. The FSIS-commissioned UCSF study (574 workers, six plants, July 2024 to January 2025) found the number of hog parts a worker handles per minute "more closely associated with MSD risk" than line speed (Federal Register 2026-03228). A cell that takes the pieces at one station removes the exposure that was measured.
  • Labour per line per shift. FSIS assumes plants running faster add 3 to 5 production workers per line per shift, costed at $38.62 per hour loaded (same document, regulatory impact analysis).
  • Yield outweighs labour. In the MLA analysis summarised in pork-processing-line.md section 8.4, about 15 percent of automation benefit was labour and 80 percent carcass value yield. The leg cell earns its keep by protecting the cut position.

Rate: US plants under NSIS on a line-speed waiver averaged 1,276 head per hour in February 2025 (same source). At 1,000 head per hour the cutting floor sees 2,000 legs per hour.

8.1Three layers: primitives, composed skills, jobs

Eighteen primitive skills cover every manipulation job in the inventory. Eight composed skills wire them with contracts: intercept_and_grasp, orient_on_belt, singulate, place_into_fixture, transfer_to_container, hang_on_hook, feature_referenced_cut, seam_pull. The leg cell builds nine of the primitives and three of the composed skills. Those nine primitives account for 134 of the 179 primitive uses across 21 jobs, 75 percent. The mapping of jobs to primitives is design judgment; what each job physically involves is sourced in the inventory.

8.2Which cell comes next

The proposed order adds as few new primitives as possible per cell, so every contract is exercised on a second product before a blade enters a cell. Each step up the chart is a cell that brings new primitives; a job counts as covered once every primitive it needs is in the library.

The first cut (stage 5) starts at the trotter with a fixed saw fed by the leg cell, so the blade stays out of the arm's tool until the cutting primitives have their own evaluation protocol. Any cutting cell is judged on yield against people on matched lots. Hindquarter deboning machines have been installed and then removed when the workers beside them out-yielded them (pork-processing-line.md section 8.3).

The first cell goes into Prestage Foods of Iowa near Eagle Grove, Wholestone's joint-venture partner. The plant's cutting floor runs Frontmatec primal cutting equipment (Food Engineering, 2019), which limits what a new cell may touch mechanically and contractually.

10.1The prediction and intercept maths, as built

The product is stuck to the belt, and an encoder measures belt travel s(t). So each camera frame k gives the product's position in belt coordinates, which stays constant while the product does not slip:

xbelt=xworld(tk)−s(tk)

Averaging those readings gives x‾belt, and the slope of the readings against time measures slip directly. The position at a future time is bookkeeping, with belt speed v from the encoder rather than from differencing images:

x^world(tf)=x‾belt+s(tlast)+v(tf−tlast)

The planner then finds the meeting time by repeating one step until it stops changing: the leg is wherever it will be when the arm arrives, and the arm needs its command delay τ plus its travel time to get there.

tmeet=tnow+τ+Tmove(x^world(tmeet)−xtool) Tmove(d)=2damax+vbeltamaxwhen d≤vmax2amax Tmove(d)=dvmax+vmaxamax+vbeltamaxotherwise

The travel time is a trapezoid speed profile with top speed vmax and acceleration amax, plus the ramp to match belt speed. A meeting is accepted only if the leg is still inside the arm's window after the jaws have closed.

What this does not handle yet: the belt changing speed. If it accelerates at a during the lead time Δt, the prediction is off by e=12aΔt2, which is 12.5 mm for every 0.1 m/s² over a 0.5 s lead † computed; the plant belt's speed changes are unmeasured. Replanning from fresh encoder readings during the approach shrinks Δt and with it that error.

10.2How each piece gets better

  1. Each stage has a fixed test set: the same legs, poses and belt speeds every time.
  2. Every run records the stage's numbers and a video, so a failure can be watched as well as counted.
  3. A change goes in, from our own research or from published work, and the same test set runs again. It stays only if the numbers improve.
  4. Yesterday's numbers become today's regression checks, so a later change cannot quietly undo an earlier one.

Section 11

Building the pipeline, one piece at a time

This is the work itself, in the order we are doing it. For each piece we say what we are trying to do, the technology we use and how it works, what we tested, and what the test showed. Where a learned method is an option, we build a geometric one first, measure both on the same data, and count what the learned one costs to run. A learned piece stays only if it earns that cost, and the two are built so they can run together, each checking the other.

11.1Placing the depth camera and looking at what it sees

Before anything can find a leg, the cell needs eyes. We put a depth camera over the belt, 950 mm up, and looked at its raw feed: a colour image, and a depth image that says how far away every pixel is.

How the technology works. An RGB-D camera of this kind is two infrared cameras a fixed distance apart, plus a projector that throws a dot pattern onto the scene so even a plain white belt has texture. The camera finds the same dot in both images; how far it shifts between them gives the distance, the same way your two eyes judge depth. Because that shift gets smaller the further away something is, the depth error grows with the square of the distance: RMS = Z² × subpixel ÷ (focal length × baseline) (Intel). At our 0.95 m that works out to about 1.2 mm on the belt.

What we tested. Two real cameras modelled from their datasheets, the Orbbec Gemini 335L and the RealSense D455, run over all 20 simulated legs in 9 positions each. We checked the simulated lens against MuJoCo's renderer (0.3 px), and compared depth noise, how many pixels land on the shank, and whether the whole leg stays in view.

11.2Segmenting the leg in simulation: geometry, then learned

Next the cell has to cut the leg out of the picture: decide which pixels are leg and which are belt, rail or arm. Everything after this, the centre of gravity, the grip point and the intercept, reads from that outline.

The geometric method. A leg stands proud of the belt, so height finds it. We record the empty belt first, then for every pixel measure how far the surface has risen above that recording. Anything between 10 and 300 mm up, inside the belt area, is leg; we keep the largest piece and refuse the frame if it is empty or runs off the image. It needs no training and no labels, and every decision can be traced to a number.

What the first runs taught us. The first run cut off every trotter hanging past the belt edge, because out there the recording saw the floor, not the belt, so the trotter looked a metre tall. The fix measured height against the belt's plane instead, but the plane fit then locked onto the floor, which fills more of this wide camera's view than the belt does. Fitting only inside the belt fixed both. On the third run every one of 180 frames came out almost exactly right: 2 wrong pixels out of 67,526.

Why that is too good, and the edge effects. The simulator draws a perfectly sharp depth edge around the leg. A real stereo camera does not: near an outline it matches its little windows across the edge, so it reports depths from the wrong side, or a mixture of near and far, and it loses surfaces seen almost edge-on. We added those three effects to the camera model: about 1 px of sideways jitter at edges (what a RealSense D435 measures below 0.7 m, Halmetschlager-Funek et al. 2019, Table 2), a 2 px band either side of the outline where half the pixels report the near depth, the far depth or a blend † assumed, and no return from surfaces tilted past 75 degrees from the camera, where the published noise fit stops (Fankhauser et al. 2015; the cut-off itself † assumed). Then we measured again on the same 180 frames.

The learned method. A U-Net (Ronneberger et al. 2015): a network that shrinks the image step by step to understand what is where, then grows it back to full size to say, pixel by pixel, whether it is leg. Ours is small, about half a million parameters, reads the colour and depth images at 320 × 200, and is trained on 990 simulated frames of 90 legs, checked on 110 frames of 10 more, none of them among the 20 test legs. It runs on the CPU, so its cost is milliseconds per frame on the cell computer, measured next to the geometric method.

What the edge effects did. The geometric mask now grows outward. It still finds every leg pixel, but about 3 percent more pixels come along: belt just outside the outline that the camera reports at the leg's height, so it passes the 10 mm cut. That moves the centre by about a millimetre, 2.4 mm at worst, still inside the 3 mm we set for the next step.

What the learned model did, and the mistake it exposed. The U-Net learned the training frames almost perfectly: 0.9998 IoU on the 10 legs held back for checking. On the 20 test legs it scored 0.968, with the centre about 3 mm off on nearly every frame, which is worse than geometry. An error that is the same size and direction on every frame is rarely the model. When we shrank the training outlines to the network's 320 × 200, we took the top-left pixel of every 4 × 4 block instead of its middle, so every label sat 1.5 px up and to the left of the leg, about 3 mm on the belt. The model learned exactly what we showed it. We checked the shift on a test shape (1.5 px before the fix, 0.04 px after), added a test so it cannot come back, and retrained both models on corrected labels.

What we decide. We wrote the rule down before training: the learned model stays only if it beats geometry by 0.01 IoU or 1 mm on the centre, with no extra refusals. The first training did not, because of the label mistake. Retrained on corrected labels, it still loses on the idealised frames, where geometry is exact, but with the camera's edge effects modelled it scores 0.984 against geometry's 0.967 with no extra refusals, so it passes. Keeping only the pixels both call leg does better again: 0.985, with the centre within 0.3 mm. So the cell uses the learned segmenter checked by the geometric one, which keeps running every frame. Training on edge-effect frames made no difference, 0.984 either way: the model did not need to see the camera's flaws to cope with them. The edge effects are a model with assumed numbers, so the real camera decides this again.

What each costs to run. On this computer's CPU, geometry took 93 to 105 ms per frame and the U-Net about 130 ms, measured while other training jobs shared the processor. Running both costs the sum, about 230 ms, against the 33 ms a 30 frames-per-second camera allows. Now that we know we keep both, making them fast is the next piece of work on this step.

11.3Segmenting real plant footage

Simulation only tells us so much, so we also ran segmentation on the real footage from the leg station: pale legs on a white modular belt, gloved hands, steel walls, legs touching. These clips come from a phone, so there is colour but no depth, and the depth method cannot run on them.

The deterministic method. A colour rule. We sampled real pixels on two frames and found that meat, skin, fat and cut faces alike, sits in a narrow band of redness and yellowness that the white belt, its shadows, the steel and the blue gloves do not. Pixels in that band are meat; each connected patch is one piece. It runs in milliseconds.

The learned method. Segment Anything (Kirillov et al. 2023), a model trained on over a billion masks to outline every distinct object in a picture, without being told what the objects are. It proposes the regions; the same colour rule then decides which regions are meat. This is the composition we want in the cell: the learned part is good at where one object ends and the next begins, and the deterministic part makes the decision we can audit.

How we judge it. There are no hand-drawn outlines for these frames, so we show both methods side by side on the same 21 frames and count, by eye, how many legs each gets as a separate, complete piece, and how long each takes.

The colour step also needs sampling on both clips, or replacing by depth once we have a real depth camera on the line.

11.4Finding the centre of gravity: geometry first

Once the leg is outlined, the cell needs its centre of gravity: the turn happens about it, and the grip has to hold against the leg's weight. The middle of the outline is not that point, because a leg's outline runs a long way down the thin hock and trotter, which weigh little.

How the geometric estimate works. The camera sees only the top of the leg. So we treat every leg pixel as a solid column of meat from the belt up to the surface the camera sees, work out each column's volume from its height and the patch of belt that pixel covers, and take the centre of all that volume. It assumes the meat is equally dense and that nothing under the surface is hollow. We compared it with the simplest answer, the centre of the outline, on the same 20 legs and 9 positions as segmentation, against the centre of mass the simulator computes from the leg's shape. The rule and the numbers we expected were written down first (experiments/2026-09-15-centre-of-gravity.md).

So the column centroid replaces the outline centre for every skill that needs the centre of gravity. It still misses the 5 mm median and 10 mm worst we proposed † gate not yet agreed. What is left is not random: the estimate sits 7 mm toward the trotter, where the round trotter lifts off the belt and the columns under it are partly air, and the error changes with where the leg lies, 5 mm when the leg is 120 mm from the far rail against 10 mm at 20 mm, and 6 mm at one angle against 11 at the other. That looks like the camera seeing one side of the leg and counting it as meat, which we have not tested yet.

The learned correction. A small network, 208,000 parameters, trained in under three minutes on 936 frames of 100 other simulated legs, looks at the leg's height on the belt around the column centroid and at where the leg sits relative to the camera, and says how far to move the centroid. On the same 180 test frames it brings the error from 7.9 mm to 1.2 mm on the median frame and from 15.7 mm to 4.6 mm at worst; with edge effects, 1.1 and 4.2 mm. The lean toward the trotter drops from 7 mm to under 1 mm. Our rule asked for at least 1 mm better without a worse worst case, so it stays, and it meets the 5 mm and 10 mm we proposed. It costs about 10 ms more per frame on the CPU (the 18 ms in the table includes the column centroid, which took 8 ms alone in that run). The geometry still runs underneath: the correction only moves the column centroid, so a move far outside what it was trained on can be seen and refused. This is a simulated leg of uniform density; a real ham's bone and fat shift its centre of mass, which only weighed legs from the line can tell us.

11.5Intercepting and grasping the leg: what goes in, what comes out

Now we use everything we have measured so far to take hold of the leg while it moves. The outline, the centre of gravity and the shape of the leg along its length, together with how fast the belt is going and whether it is speeding up or slowing down, go into a grasp policy. The policy says where on the leg to grip and how, and nothing about which robot does it. Then each arm works out for itself how to get there on time. That is what lets the same grasp skill run on the SCARA and the six-axis arm, and on the next robot too, and it is how we find out whether the tool needs to tilt.

What goes in. One message per leg, LegPerception: when the frame was taken and how far the belt had moved; the centre of gravity; the outline centre; the leg's direction from ham to trotter, found as the direction from the centre of gravity toward the outline centre, which always sits 40 mm toward the thin end (11.4); its length and volume; and 40 cross sections along it, each with its middle, width and height. Beside it, BeltState: travel, speed and acceleration fitted to the last 0.3 s of encoder readings.

What comes out. GraspAction: the point between the closed jaws, on the leg, in belt coordinates, so it rides with the belt; the line the jaws close along; how far the tool leans; how wide to open; how long to close. No joint angles and no robot name.

The deterministic policy. Grip the shank two thirds of the way from the ham, at the middle of that cross section, jaws square to the leg there, opening the shank's width plus 40 mm. On a simulated leg at two angles, working only from the camera, it lands within 20 mm of the true shank point and within 8 degrees of square to it. The old grasp skills read the simulator's true pose, so this is the first grasp the cell can decide from what it sees.

The learned policy. It reads the same message and writes the same action. It does not start from nothing: it takes the deterministic grasp and learns only how far to move it along and across the leg, how far to turn the jaws, and whether to tilt the tool 0, 15 or 30 degrees. With nothing learned it is exactly the deterministic grasp. It is trained on 100 simulated legs the test never uses, each labelled with the best of a set of grasps tried on a floating gripper, and it runs as plain matrix maths so it fits inside a ROS 2 node without a GPU or torch.

The intercept. From the grasp point and the belt state, the planner finds the moment the arm can arrive exactly where that point will be, using the belt's acceleration so a belt that is speeding up or slowing down does not leave the arm reaching for where the leg used to be (10.1); it waits if the leg is not in reach yet, and says why when the leg would leave before the jaws close. The arm then comes down along its own axis onto the moving point and follows it while the jaws close.

ROS 2 and MoveIt. The messages are a ROS 2 package, and three nodes carry the chain: belt state, grasp policy (picked by a parameter) and one executor per arm, which asks for the joint angles at each waypoint and sends one timed trajectory to the arm's controller. MoveIt is now installed on this computer, and the arm descriptions it uses are generated from the simulator's own numbers: they match the simulated arms within 0.1 mm, and MoveIt's answers for the UR20 put the tool exactly where asked, tilted or not. A SCARA's tool can only point down, so its joint angles come from a short closed-form solver that answers the same request MoveIt does; the executor cannot tell the difference. Adding a robot means adding its description and config; the policy, messages and planner stay as they are.

First runs, end to end. The simulated cell now runs as ROS 2 nodes too: the camera's leg goes out as a message, the policy decides, the executor plans and solves, the simulated arm follows the trajectory, and the simulator scores the result against the truth. The first runs found five defects in the chain, all fixed; the worst was stray pixels at the leg's outline that made the leg look twice as long and put every grasp 155 mm off.
