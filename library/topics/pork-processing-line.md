# The pork processing line, the cutting floor, and the leg

Written to answer one question: where does an intercept-and-align station for whole
bone-in pork legs sit in a pork plant, what happens upstream of it, what happens
downstream, and what does the product actually look like when it arrives.

The customer video shows workers hand-placing whole bone-in legs, hock and trotter
attached, onto a white modular plastic belt, orienting each one, and feeding a cutter.
That places the station on the **cutting floor**, downstream of chilling, at the point
where legs separated from the carcass are presented to a saw or to a deboning line. It
is the last hand-placement before a machine takes over. Everything below is organised
around that.

Companion entries: [meat cutting automation](meat-cutting-automation.md),
[meat piece properties](meat-piece-properties.md),
[meat cell architecture](meat-cell-architecture.md).

## 1. The station in one table

| Stage | Where the leg is | Temperature | Who does it today |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slaughter and dressing | Attached to the carcass, on an overhead rail | Body temperature, falling | Mixed: stunning, scalding, dehairing, splitting automated; evisceration largely manual |
| Chilling | Still on the carcass, in a tunnel then an equalisation room | Deep ham reaches 2 C at 21 h (95 kg carcass) to 28 h (105 kg) | Fully automated conveyance |
| Laydown and primal cutting | Separated from the side, on a belt or in a bin | 0 to 4 C typical, 7 C legal max | Automated primal saws in large plants, manual elsewhere |
| **Leg presentation (this station)** | **Loose, singulated, on a white modular belt** | **0 to 4 C typical, 7 C legal max** | **Manual, everywhere** |
| Trimming, aitch bone, shank, dressing | On a belt or a hanging line | 0 to 4 C | Manual knife work, some automated pullers |
| Deboning | On a belt, hanging line, or in a HAMDAS-type machine | 0 to 4 C | Overwhelmingly manual |
| Product route | Dry-cure, cooked ham, fresh, further processing | Varies | Mixed |

## 3. The process, stage by stage

### 3.1 Lairage, stunning, bleeding

Large EU pork plants use group CO2 stunning: pigs are driven into a gondola that
descends into a well of carbon dioxide. Electrical stunning, head-only or head-to-body,
is the alternative and is more common in smaller plants. Vendors: Frontmatec
([stunning](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/unclean-line/stunning/)),
JBT Marel ([pig stunning](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/pig-pork/pig-stunning/)), Butina.

Sticking and bleeding follow immediately. EU law requires stunning and bleeding to be
separated in space or time from the scalding, depilation, scraping and singeing
operations, and from evisceration and further dressing
([Reg. 853/2004 Annex III Section I Chapter II](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2004/853/annex/III)).
This separation is why a plant has physically distinct rooms, and it constrains where a
robot cell can be placed.

**Automation status:** stunning and conveyance automated, sticking mostly manual.

### 3.2 Scalding, dehairing, singeing, polishing

Pigs are not skinned. The hide stays on, so bristle removal is a separate problem that
cattle and sheep lines do not have. Scalding softens the follicles, a rotary dehairer
with rubber flails beats the bristles off, a gas flame singes the remainder, and
polishing brushes finish the surface.

The regulation is explicit: "When not skinned, porcine animals must have their bristles
removed immediately. The risk of contamination of the meat with scalding water must be
minimised. Only approved additives may be used for this operation. Porcine animals must
be thoroughly rinsed afterwards with potable water"
([Reg. 853/2004 Annex III Section I Chapter IV](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2004/853/annex/III)).

The process parameters, all sourced:

| Step | Parameter | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Immersion scald | **58 to 65 C for 3 to 6 min**; longer than 6 min at that temperature damages the skin | [EU BREF for Slaughterhouses, §2.1.2.6](https://bureau-industrial-transformation.jrc.ec.europa.eu/sites/default/files/2025-01/sa_bref_0505-bref.pdf) |
| Immersion scald, measured in plants | 60 C for 7 min (Belgium); 60 C for 5 to 10 min (Ireland); 7.5 min at 58.5 to 62 C and 10 min at 62 C (two Irish abattoirs) | [Pathogens 2021;10(1):77](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7831019/), [Ir Vet J 2014;67:1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3902064/) |
| Immersion scald, regulator target | Tank at a minimum of 60 C reduces bacterial load "by as much as 4-log"; **61 C or higher for at least 8 minutes** may be adequate | [FSIS Salmonella in Swine Slaughter Guideline, 2023, p.25](https://www.usda.gov/sites/default/files/guidance-documents/FSIS.%20FSIS%20Guideline%20to%20Control%20Salmonella%20in%20Swine%20Slaughter%20and%20Pork%20Processing%20Establishments.pdf) |
| Condensation / steam scald | Humidified air at **about 60 to 62 C at 100% humidity**; FSIS gives a vertical steam tunnel at minimum 61 C for 6 to 7 min, with **internal meat temperature not exceeding 41 C** | BREF §4.2.2.3.1; FSIS Guideline pp.25-26 |
| Dehairing | Rotating rubber flails, water spray that continues the scald; FSIS best practice is **60 to 62 C** water in the machine | BREF §2.1.2.7; FSIS Guideline p.26 |
| Singeing | Flame **900 to 1,000 C**, exposure **5 to 15 s** per carcass; a typical unit has 40 burners firing 5 s per carcass, about 20 s in any minute | BREF §2.1.2.8 |
| Singeing, measured carcass surface | 12 s after the flame: min 53.9 to 60.8 C, mean 57.5 to 68.0 C, max 61.0 to 75.4 C, from a 32 C entry temperature | [Rev Bras Saúde Prod Anim 2022;23, doi:10.1590/S1519-9940202100372022](https://doi.org/10.1590/S1519-9940202100372022) |
| Polishing | Rotating rubber flails plus water, to cool the carcass, soften the skin and rinse debris; 260 to 460 L per tonne of carcass | BREF §2.1.2.9 |

Water is the reason steam scalding is an EU best available technique. On a 2,400 pigs/day
basis the BREF compares a scalding tank at **8,440,000 L/year** against condensation
scalding at **336,000 L/year**, a 25-fold difference, with daily pump and fan energy also
falling from 240 to 176 kWh. The BREF is honest about the economics: replacing an existing
tank "is not considered to be profitable based on the water and energy savings alone", so
it happens at major rebuild or greenfield only.

One fact worth carrying forward, because it shapes where anything touching product may
sit: **dehairing is a contamination step, not a cleaning step.** It raises aerobic colony
count by **1.94 log10 CFU/cm²** and Enterobacteriaceae prevalence by a risk ratio of
**17.36 (95% CI 6.88 to 43.75)**
([Foods 2022;11(14):2110](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9315615/)). In one
plant, **100% of carcasses were Salmonella-positive after dehairing against 16.7% after
bleeding**, because recycled dehairer water measured only 28 to 38 C, warm enough for
Salmonella to grow ([Pathogens 2021;10(1):77](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7831019/)).
Singeing is what claws it back, at 2.5 to 3.0 log10 CFU/cm², and then polishing gives some
of it away again: FSIS calls polishing "a primary mode of pork carcass recontamination
after singeing" and recommends a second singe after it (FSIS Guideline pp.28, 30).

Vendors split this into scalding and dehairing modules
([JBT Marel](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/pig-pork/scalding-and-dehairing/),
[Frontmatec](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/unclean-line/scalding-dehairing/)),
then washing, polishing and singeing as final carcass treatment
([JBT Marel](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/pig-pork/final-carcass-treatment/)).
Note that BANSS is now inside Frontmatec and its technical datasheets have been withdrawn
from the public web. A "Marel SmoothFlow scalding tunnel" does not exist; the Marel
scalding products are Turn-O-Matic, the spray water system, and the transversal tank
([JBT Marel](https://jbtmarel.com/en/products/transversal-tank-for-swine-scalding/)).

**Skin-off is the minority route.** EU law recognises it by implication, requiring bristle
removal only "when not skinned"
([Reg. 853/2004 Annex III Sec. I Ch. IV](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2004/853/annex/III)),
and the BREF states plainly that "the practice of de-hiding pigs is relatively rare", at
about 70 L of water per pig where it is done (§3.1.2.3). Skin-on also wins on yield:
carcass weights and yields were "noticeably higher after singeing" than after
scalding-dehairing, with **hams heavier in singed carcasses**
([Meat Sci 1995;39(2):247](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22059830/)). If the customer's
legs have rind on, this is why.

**Automation status:** fully automated. These are machines that act on the whole carcass
with no need to locate anatomy precisely, which is exactly the property the leg station
does not have.

### 3.3 Evisceration and dressing

The bung is dropped, the belly is opened, the viscera are removed onto an inspection
table or a synchronised tray system, and the carcass is trimmed. This is where the first
real robots appear. Frontmatec's AiRA range includes a bung dropper (RBD), a combined
bung dropper and aitch bone cutter (RBDH), a standalone aitch bone cutter (RHC), a belly
and breast opener (RBO), a neck clipper (RNC), and a leaf lard remains remover (RLR)
([Frontmatec AiRA robots](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/clean-line-chill-room/aira-robots/)).
JBT Marel calls their equivalent the M-line robots
([JBT Marel](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/pig-pork/dressing-cutting-and-evisceration/m-line-robots/)).

**Automation status:** partly automated and getting more so. Note that these robots work
on a carcass hanging from a rail, which gives them a strong pose prior: gravity fixes
the orientation and the rail fixes the position along one axis. The leg station has no
such prior.

### 3.4 Splitting

The carcass is split down the vertebral column with a splitting saw. Frontmatec sells
robotic splitters with a saw (RPS-S), with knives (RPS-H), and a dual-arm version
(RPS-D) for high capacity
([Frontmatec AiRA robots](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/clean-line-chill-room/aira-robots/)).

**Automation status:** automated in any plant above modest scale.

### 3.5 Inspection

Post-mortem inspection is a legal requirement, not a processing step. In the EU it is
governed by [Reg. 2019/627](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2019/627/contents) and is
largely visual for routine pigs, with Trichinella testing under
[Reg. 2015/1375](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2015/1375/contents). In the US, FSIS
has been moving in the same direction; a rule titled "Visual Post-Mortem Inspection in
Swine Slaughter Establishments" was published on
[19 August 2025](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/08/19/2025-15749/visual-post-mortem-inspection-in-swine-slaughter-establishments)
and again on
[21 May 2026](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/05/21/2026-10186/visual-post-mortem-inspection-in-swine-slaughter-establishments).

**Automation status:** manual, and will stay manual. It is a regulated human judgement.

### 3.6 Chilling

This is the step that decides what temperature the leg is at when it reaches the
station, so it is worth getting the numbers right.

EU law: "post-mortem inspection must be followed immediately by chilling in the
slaughterhouse to ensure a temperature throughout the meat of not more than 3 °C for
offal and 7 °C for other meat along a chilling curve that ensures a continuous decrease
of the temperature"
([Reg. 853/2004 Annex III Section I Chapter VII](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2004/853/annex/III)).
The same annex permits meat to be cut and boned during chilling, and separately permits
cutting before the temperature is reached when the cutting room is on the same site as
the slaughter premises.

Two chilling regimes exist. **Conventional chilling** puts carcasses straight into a
chill room at around 0 to 4 C. **Blast or shock chilling** runs them through a tunnel at
a much lower air temperature for a short period first, then into an equalisation room.
Vendors sell exactly this pair: a "quick chill tunnel" that "reduces drip-loss to a
minimum" and a separate "equalization chill room"
([Frontmatec cooling](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/clean-line-chill-room/cooling/)).

Measured numbers, from a blast-chilled commercial abattoir, n = 754 carcasses, internal
loin and ham temperature logged at 45 time points from 40 to 1,320 min post mortem:
hams of 95 kg hot-carcass-weight carcasses were projected to reach 2 C in **21 h**, and
hams of 105 kg carcasses in **28 h**; loins reached 2 C in 17 h and 27 h respectively
([Overholt et al. 2019, J Anim Sci, doi:10.1093/jas/skz131](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC6541802)).

For legs specifically, a study of an industrial blast chiller used for PDO Parma ham
production measured apparent heat transfer coefficients of **27.1 W m^-2 C^-1** in the
fastest cooling zone and **15.6 W m^-2 C^-1** in the slowest, and set a HACCP critical
control limit of **11 C at 5 mm depth within 2 h** of the start of cooling
([Rinaldi, Cordioli, Barbanti 2017, J Food Sci Technol, doi:10.1007/s13197-016-2397-3](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC5305697)).

The Parma production specification describes the same step from the plant's side: after
slaughter the leg is separated from the side and held in cold rooms for 24 h, "to bring
the leg temperature from the animal's body temperature to 0 °C" and "because cold
temperatures harden the meat that can be trimmed more easily"
([Prosciutto di Parma specification, 17 May 2025](https://www.prosciuttodiparma.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Prosciutto_di_Parma_Specification_17_05_2025.pdf)).
The Italian disciplinare adds three constraints that pin down the state of the product at
the station: freezing is **prohibited**, storage and transport must hold internal
temperature **between -1 °C and +4 °C**, and legs may not be used if they come from pigs
slaughtered **less than 24 hours or more than 120 hours** earlier
([disciplinare, 06/03/2023](https://www.prosciuttodiparma.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/Disciplinare_Prosciutto_di_Parma_06_03_2023_ITA.pdf)).
Independent measurements on the same chain report green hams "chilled (0-2 °C) for 24 h,
trimmed to obtain the typical round ham shape, and weighed again", then moved to the ham
factory "within two days after the slaughter"
([doi:10.3390/ani11082447](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11082447),
[doi:10.3390/ani12020215](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani12020215)).

So the leg at the station is between one and five days post mortem, between -1 and +4 C,
never frozen, and has already been weighed at least once.

The practical consequence for a robot: a leg presented on the cutting floor is cold and
stiff, not warm and floppy. It has been chilled for at least a shift. Its surface is
tacky rather than slick. Stiffness helps a gripper and helps pose repeatability.

**Automation status:** fully automated conveyance, no human contact.

### 3.7 Carcass grading

Grading decides which cuts the carcass is allocated to, which is upstream of everything
the cutting floor does. Frontmatec's AutoFom III is an automatic ultrasound scanner that
estimates lean content and individual cut weights
([Frontmatec carcass grading](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/unclean-line/carcass-grading-traceability/),
[JBT Marel carcass grading](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/pig-pork/carcass-grading/)).
EU carcasses are classified on the SEUROP scale by estimated lean meat percentage.

**Automation status:** fully automated where installed.

### 3.10 Product routes

A leg leaves the cutting floor on one of four routes.

- **Fresh bone-in or boneless leg.** Sold as a roasting joint or further cut. IMPS items
  401 through 402K define the trade forms
  ([USDA AMS IMPS 400, Fresh Pork](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/IMPS_400_Fresh_Pork%5B1%5D.pdf)).
- **Dry-cured, bone-in.** Parma, San Daniele, Serrano, Ibérico, Bayonne. The leg is
  trimmed, salted, rested, washed, dried, and matured for 12 to 36 months.
- **Cooked ham.** The leg is deboned, the muscles are separated or kept in a block,
  brine is injected, the meat is tumbled, then moulded and cooked. This is where most
  industrial ham volume goes.
- **Further processing.** Trim into sausage, ground product, or a formed product.

Bacon comes from the belly (US style) or the loin (UK back bacon), not from the leg.

## 4. The cutting floor in detail

### 4.1 How a side becomes primals

A split side is separated into leg (ham), loin, belly, and shoulder, with the head and
jowl handled separately. The two specification systems that define the cuts are the
North American Institutional Meat Purchase Specifications, maintained by the Meat
Institute, AMSA and USDA AMS
([USDA AMS IMPS](https://www.ams.usda.gov/grades-standards/imps)), and the UNECE
Standard for Porcine Meat, Carcases and Cuts, for European trade. The two differ in
where the shoulder separates from the loin and belly: North American cutting generally
separates between the first and second rib or between the second and third rib, while
pork produced for Asian, European or South American markets generally separates between
the fourth and fifth rib
([Transl Anim Sci 2026, doi:10.1093/tas/txag024](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12978301)).

The IMPS definition of the leg cut is the precise one worth memorising, because it
defines the piece the station handles:

> "The leg is separated from the side by a straight cut approximately perpendicular to a
> line parallel to the shank bones. The cut passes through a point which is not less than
> 1.5 inches (3.8 cm) from the anterior edge of the aitch bone. The foot shall be removed
> at or slightly above the hock joint."
> ([IMPS 400, Item No. 401](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/IMPS_400_Fresh_Pork%5B1%5D.pdf))

Note that IMPS 401 has the foot removed. The customer's legs have the trotter attached,
which rules out a US fresh-leg programme and points at a dry-cure or export route.
See section 5.3.

Two more differences matter before reading any vendor number:

- **Carcass presentation differs.** US Item 400 is dressed "packer style", **without head
  and kidneys**. EU carcases are weighed **head-on**: Reg. (EU) 1308/2013 Annex IV Part B
  requires them "without tongue, bristles, hooves, genital organs, flare fat, kidneys and
  diaphragm", and classifies them SEUROP by lean meat percentage (S at or above 60%, E 55
  to 60, U 50 to 55, R 45 to 50, O 40 to 45, P below 40)
  ([Reg. 1308/2013 Annex IV](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2013/1308/annex/IV)).
  So a "110 kg carcass" means different things in Kansas and in Parma.
- **The leg separation point differs.** A vendor states the adjustment concretely: the
  hind-leg/middle splitting position runs "from 1.5 inches straight vertebra (EU
  specification) to 1 inch from the aitchbone (US specification)"
  ([JBT Marel DeboFlex Primal Cut](https://jbtmarel.com/en/news/deboflex-primal-cut-simplifying-primal-cutting-with-vertical-precision)).
  IMPS 401 itself specifies not less than 1.5 in from the aitch bone edge, so treat the
  vendor's "1 inch" as one customer's spec rather than the IMPS minimum.

Frontmatec's AGOL line divides a half carcass into **leg, middle and shoulder**, cutting
"due to measuring of the pork carcass between the hipbone and the shoulder muscle"
([Frontmatec AGOL EU](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/primal-cutting/automatic-primal-cutting/automatic-primal-cutting-line-eu/)).
That three-way split, not the US four-primal split, is the one the customer's floor most
likely runs.

At 1,000 pigs per hour, 2,000 legs per hour reach the cutting floor, which is one leg
every 1.8 s. A station handling a single leg lane at a large plant therefore has a takt
of roughly 2 to 4 s depending on how many parallel lanes the plant runs. Note that the
HAMDAS-RX takt is 7.2 s per ham at 500/hour, and the 1999 prototype ran a 12 s cycle
(10 s dwell plus 2 s transfer) at 300 hams/hour across 17 stations
([ICoMST 1999](https://digicomst.ie/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/1999_02_03.pdf)). A single
robot cell replacing a single operator is competing against a human at roughly that rate,
not against the slaughter line rate.

**Headcount on a cutting floor:** no vendor or regulator publishes a per-floor headcount,
and any figure of the form "N people per cutting floor" is unverified. Three sourced
proxies exist.

- **Cost.** A pork fabrication cost model assumes **US$33 per carcass in labour**, against
  US$14 overhead and US$12 packaging, US$59 per carcass total fixed cost, and notes these
  hourly workers (live animal handlers, slaughter floor, cutting floor, packaging) are
  costed per carcass rather than per kilogram
  ([Transl Anim Sci 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12978301)).
- **Per machine.** The HAMDAS 1999 prototype needed **seven manual preparation workers
  including one loader** to feed a 300 legs/hour machine
  ([ICoMST 1999](https://digicomst.ie/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/1999_02_03.pdf)). The
  current HAMDAS-RX still needs preparation, rework and an operator (per the European
  line diagram, which could not be re-verified from this network). Even the most
  automated leg deboner in the world is fed by people.
- **Work rate.** In one customer case study, the manual deboning line ran **60+ actions
  per minute** per operator against about **20 actions per minute** on the automated
  hanging line, "below the legal maximum of 30"
  ([JBT Marel, Alibem](https://jbtmarel.com/en/customer-stories/alibem-automates-deboning-and-raises-yield-with-deboflex/)).

On ergonomics specifically, a study of ham boning measured torque and repetition across
22 surveys on three production lines and found operators "managed a microbreak in the
cycle ranging from **0.88% to 4%** of the survey time", with operators reporting no
symptoms averaging longer microbreaks
([Stoy and Aspen 1999, *AAOHN Journal* 47(6), doi:10.1177/216507999904700603](https://doi.org/10.1177/216507999904700603)).
Under 4% recovery time in a cycle is the human cost of this job, and it is the strongest
argument for automating the station.

## 5. The leg line

### 5.1 What happens to a leg, in order

1. **Separation.** The leg comes off the side at the cut defined above, not less than
   3.8 cm anterior to the aitch bone edge, perpendicular to the shank bones.
2. **Foot decision.** Removed at or slightly above the hock joint for the IMPS fresh
   leg; retained for Spanish dry-cure.
3. **Facing and trimming.** Skin and collar fat over the inside muscle (semimembranosus)
   are trimmed "smooth and well rounded". The leg is "faced" by a cut that exposes the
   semitendinosus and a cross section of the ball of the femur
   ([IMPS 400 items 401 and 401E](https://www.ams.usda.gov/sites/default/files/media/IMPS_400_Fresh_Pork%5B1%5D.pdf)).
   Maximum fat thickness beneath the leg face is scheduled by weight class: 3.8 cm for
   7.7 to 9.1 kg legs, 4.3 cm for 9.1 to 12.7 kg, 5.0 cm for 12.7 kg and up.
4. **Aitch bone (pelvic bone) removal.** Item 401C is the leg skinned with the pelvic
   bone, oyster, vertebrae, flank and ilio psoas removed. On a slaughter line the aitch
   bone cut is now sometimes done by robot before the leg is separated
   ([Frontmatec AiRA RHC](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/clean-line-chill-room/aira-robots/)).
5. **Shank cutting.** Item 401A is the short-shank leg, cut at an approximate right angle
   to the shank bones exposing a cross section of the gastrocnemius. Item 401D is the
   hind shank, separated through the stifle joint.
6. **Skinning.** Item 402 is the skinned leg, with the skin removed anterior to a line
   parallel to the leg face starting at a point not exceeding 25% of the distance from
   the stifle joint to the leg face, and remaining fat trimmed to not more than 13 mm.
7. **Weight grading and batching.** Legs are weighed individually and sorted into weight
   bands, because every downstream route has a weight specification
   ([JBT Marel grading and batching](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/pig-pork/grading-and-batching/)).
8. **Route split.** Dry-cure, cooked ham, fresh, or further processing.

### 5.2 Deboning order

The bones in a whole leg are the pelvic bone (aitch or hip), the femur (leg bone), the
patella (kneecap), the tibia and fibula (shank and splint bones), and the tarsals and
metatarsals if the foot is on.

Mayekawa published the conventional manual sequence when they presented the first HAMDAS
prototype at ICoMST in 1999, and it is the best primary statement of the cut order in
print ([45th ICoMST 1999, pp. 50-59](https://digicomst.ie/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/1999_02_03.pdf)):

1. Cut the Achilles tendon and strip the muscle off the hind shank along the hind shank bone.
2. Cut off the exposed hind shank bone at the knee joint.
3. Loosen the tail bone on both sides.
4. Strip the muscle remaining on the hip bone by pushing toward the tail bone, then loosen the hip bone while exposing its surface.
5. Gouge the muscle in the cavity of the pubic bone and cut off the hip bone from the inside ham.
6. Cut the hip joint out and cut into the back side of the hip bone, then remove hip bone and tail bone.
7. Cut the muscle along the membrane between the inside ham and the knuckle.
8. Cut up from the bottom of the leg bone joint and remove the leg bone by pulling at the end.
9. Remove the kneecap bone.

So the order is **shank end first, then hip, then femur**, not hip-first as the bare
anatomy might suggest. The shank is opened first because it gives a rigid handle and
exposes the knee joint, which is the reference for everything after it.

The machine reorders this. HAMDAS-RX requires the hipbone to be removed before the leg
enters ("Hipbone must be removed prior to deboning"), then works from the ankle inward
([Mayekawa HAMDAS spec sheet](https://americas.mayekawa.com/mna/downloads/pdf/Food%20Robotics/HAMDAS.pdf)).
The prototype's automated station sequence was: peripheral cut around the ankle, hind
shank muscle ripping, splint bone length measurement, semicircular cut on the splint bone
side at the knee joint, semicircular cut on the leg bone side, peripheral cut around the
knee joint end of the leg bone, peripheral cut below the kneecap, muscle stripping along
the leg bone with a second measurement, final separation at the end of the leg bone, then
eject the waste bone. Leg bone, hind shank bone and kneecap come off as one connected
piece, because the knee joint is never cut (ICoMST 1999, above).

Two details in that paper are worth carrying into any cut-path policy. First, the machine
**measures each piece twice** (splint bone length, then again during muscle stripping)
rather than trusting a single initial estimate. Second, the failure mode is cascading: if
the first marking cut is poor and knee-joint detection fails, "built-in microprocessors at
the following work stations cannot control positioning of the round cutter blades,
resulting in total failure of the mechanical deboning process". The first reference cut
carries the whole sequence.

Watch [PorkSeams](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vXAWt98DCI0) and
[De-boning a pork leg](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=G-9csjIC5uA) back to back to see
the manual order executed.

Bone is **7.95 to 8.18%** of the ham by measured tissue dissection (section 6.1.1) and
**9.60%** of the untrimmed ham primal by USDA cutting-yield assumption, which also counts
the aitch bone
([Transl Anim Sci 2026, Table 12](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12978301)).

### 5.3 Why the trotter is there, and what it means

The customer's legs have hock and trotter attached. That is a route signal.

The premise that "the trotter is left on for dry-cured ham" turns out not to hold
uniformly, and the split runs along national lines rather than along the fresh/cured line.

- **Spanish DOP hams keep it.** The Consejo Regulador of DOP Jamón de Teruel describes
  the product as **"conservando la pata"**, presented with all the rind or profiled in a
  V cut, minimum weight 7 kg, minimum elaboration 60 weeks, cured at 800 m altitude or
  above ([Jamón de Teruel](https://jamondeteruel.com/es/jamon-de-teruel/)). This is the
  clearest primary statement found that the foot stays on.
- **The Spanish national quality norm does not mandate it.** Real Decreto 4/2014 requires
  processing on "piezas osteomusculares íntegras" (whole bone-in osteomuscular pieces) and
  sets minimum finished weights of **5.75 kg for jamón 100% ibérico and 7 kg for jamón
  ibérico**, minimum elaboration of **600 days below 7 kg and 730 days at or above 7 kg**,
  and minimum carcass weights of **115 kg (108 kg for 100% ibérico)**
  ([Real Decreto 4/2014](https://www.boe.es/buscar/act.php?id=BOE-A-2014-318)). A
  full-text search of the consolidated BOE text finds no hoof or *pezuña* requirement.
  Keeping the foot is a DOP and trade convention, not a national legal requirement.
- **Parma removes it.** The finished PDO product is defined as having a "rounded shape:
  without the distal part (the trotter)"
  ([Prosciutto di Parma specification](https://www.prosciuttodiparma.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/08/Prosciutto_di_Parma_Specification_17_05_2025.pdf)).
  Italian research hams are likewise "trimmed to obtain the typical shape of Veneto ham,
  without the trotter" ([doi:10.3390/ani12020215](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani12020215)).
- **US fresh legs remove it** at or slightly above the hock joint, per IMPS 401.

The usual explanations for the Spanish practice, that the foot is a hanging point through
a 600 to 730 day cure and a breed-authentication cue at the point of sale, appear only in
marketing copy. **(Unverified: no primary document found stating the reason.)** The
observable fact, which is what matters here, is that the customer's legs have it.

The engineering consequence: a leg with hock and trotter attached is longer, has a thin
rigid distal end with very little mass, and has an obvious natural handle. A gripper
that clamps the shank or the trotter has a rigid, low-variance feature to hold. A gripper
that clamps the butt end is holding soft, wet, variable tissue.

### 6.1.1 What is inside the leg

Tissue composition of the ham, n = 140 Polish commercial fatteners
([Knecht and Duziński 2016, *Archives Animal Breeding* 59:51-57, Table 2](https://www.arch-anim-breed.net/59/51/2016/aab-59-51-2016.pdf)):

| Tissue | Share of ham |
|---|---|
| Muscle | 68.11 to 69.49% |
| Skin with subcutaneous fat | 18.38 to 19.53% |
| **Bone** | **7.95 to 8.18%** |
| Intermuscular fat | 3.85 to 4.32% |

The same study puts the ham at **24.17 to 24.59% of the half carcass** (gilts 24.59
+/- 1.07%, barrows 24.17 +/- 1.02%, P at or below 0.01 for sex), falling with backfat
class from 24.88 +/- 1.13% below 22 mm to 23.88 +/- 0.88% above 26 mm, and falling with
EUROP class from 25.94% at S to 23.25% at R. That independently corroborates the 24.59%
USDA figure in section 4.1, and the 8% bone fraction matches the 86.6 to 88.2% deboning
yields measured on the HAMDAS prototype (section 8.2).

For a gripper, the numbers that matter are: roughly 8% rigid bone by mass, concentrated
along one axis; roughly 19% skin and subcutaneous fat forming a tough outer layer; and
about 70% muscle, which is the compliant part.

### 6.2 Dimensions

No published table of whole bone-in pork leg length, width and thickness was found.
This is a real gap, not an oversight. What is available is indirect:

- Maximum fat thickness beneath the leg face is **3.8 to 5.0 cm** depending on weight
  class, which bounds the soft layer a gripper compresses through (IMPS 400 item 401).
- Fat covering measured from the tip of the thighbone is not less than **22 mm** for
  Parma-grade legs (Parma specification).
- Measured subcutaneous fat depth on Italian green hams: **28.2 to 32.4 mm** at the
  biceps femoris (P1), **6.4 to 7.2 mm** at the semimembranosus (P2)
  ([doi:10.3390/ani11082447](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani11082447)). A five-fold
  difference in soft-layer thickness across one piece, which is the single most useful
  dimensional fact here for grasp force planning.
- Short trim leaves at most **9 cm** of exposed muscle beyond the femur head, which
  bounds the butt-end face (Parma specification).
- The shank bone left exposed on a semi-boneless "steamship" leg is not less than
  **8.8 cm** (IMPS 400 item 401E), which gives a sense of shank length.
- The 1.5 in (3.8 cm) offset from the aitch bone edge defines where the butt face sits
  relative to the pelvis (IMPS 400 item 401).
- Crust freezers accept products up to **200 mm** in height
  ([JBT Marel](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/ultra-fast-meat-crust-freezing/)), which is a
  weak upper bound on the thickness of pieces that move through this part of a plant.

Everything else, including an overall length, is unverified and should be measured. With
trotter on, the piece is long and asymmetric: dense bone at one end, wide soft butt at
the other, with the centre of mass well toward the butt. This asymmetry is the dominant
fact for grasp planning and is the reason a single centre-of-bounding-box grasp will
fail. See [grasp selection for soft slabs](grasp-selection-for-soft-slabs.md) for the
beam mechanics, and [meat piece properties](meat-piece-properties.md) for density.

Recommended field measurement: 50 legs, calipered for maximum length including trotter,
maximum width at the butt, maximum thickness, and mass, logged with the plant's grading
weight. That single dataset removes the largest unknown in the cell.

### 6.3 Temperature and surface state

- **Not warm.** Hot boning exists but is not the normal route for legs, and the leg
  station sits after chilling.
- EU cutting rooms hold meat at **not more than 7 C** by means of an **ambient
  temperature of not more than 12 C**
  ([Reg. 853/2004 Annex III Section I Chapter V](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2004/853/annex/III)).
  So the room is around 10 to 12 C and the product is at or below 7 C.
- Deep ham reaches 2 C somewhere between **21 and 28 h** post mortem in a blast-chilled
  plant, depending on carcass weight
  ([Overholt et al. 2019](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC6541802)).
- **Crust freezing** is applied to meat before slicing, to surface-stabilise it so the
  portion cutter makes a clean cut, with a residence time "typically 3 to 5 minutes,
  depending on product size" for products up to 200 mm in height
  ([JBT Marel crust freezing](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/ultra-fast-meat-crust-freezing/)).
  It is applied to portioned product, not to whole bone-in legs, so the station should
  **not** expect a frozen crust. Target crust depth in mm and target surface temperature
  are not published on the vendor page and are unverified.

The practical robot-facing consequences: cold meat is stiffer and holds pose better; the
surface is wet but not warm-greasy; condensation forms on anything colder than the room
including camera windows; and any gripper elastomer has to keep its properties at 2 C.

## 7. Handling and presentation

### 7.1 How pieces move

- **Overhead rail and gambrel** for whole carcasses, from stunning through chilling. This
  is the plant's backbone and it gives every carcass a known position and orientation.
- **Laydown** is the transition point. A machine takes the carcass off the hook and lays
  it down "for correct working position" and "product alignment for further processing"
  ([Frontmatec laydown](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/primal-cutting/laydown/)).
  After laydown, pose information is lost and has to be re-established by a person or a
  camera. **This is the structural reason the station exists.**
- **Modular plastic belt conveyors** for primals and loose pieces on the cutting floor.
- **Hanging deboning lines** as an alternative to belts for legs and shoulders, where the
  piece hangs from a hook and indexes past operators
  ([Frontmatec hanging ham line](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=a3JaVILyamQ),
  [JBT Marel DeboFlex](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/deboflex-transforming-pork-cutting-and-deboning/)).
- **Bins, dolavs and combo bins** for trim and bulk. USDA market reporting prices product
  by packaging type including "combo bins (i.e., high capacity bins designed to be moved
  by forklifts or pallet jacks)"
  ([Transl Anim Sci 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12978301)).

### 7.2 Why white modular plastic belt

The belt in the customer video is the standard for this room. The reasons, in order of
how well sourced they are:

- **Cleanability and food contact.** Modular plastic belting is sold specifically for
  hygienic conveyance in meat. Intralox markets Series 800 Flat Top with Heavy Duty Edge
  for meat and poultry in polyketone, and ThermoDrive as a solid thermoplastic,
  tensionless, positive-drive belt that avoids "hygienic risks posed by fabric belts or
  exposed fabric or yarn reinforcements"
  ([Intralox meat and poultry](https://www.intralox.com/industries/food/meat-poultry),
  [ThermoDrive](https://www.intralox.com/products/thermodrive)).
- **Sanitation time.** Vendor case studies claim ThermoDrive "halves cleaning time and
  water consumption", with a Silver Fern Farms case study quoted at "25 hours of cleaning
  saved annually" and a Salumificio Mec Palmieri case study at "75% less cleaning time"
  ([Intralox meat and poultry](https://www.intralox.com/industries/food/meat-poultry)).
  These are vendor claims, not independent measurements.
- **Foreign material control.** Belt fragments are a known contamination source, and
  detectable belt materials are sold to address it
  ([Intralox FoodSafe](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=EWBEfPpVD3w)).
- **The series for this room is Intralox S800**, pitch 50.8 mm, natural acetal,
  13,100 N/m, belt mass 12.3 kg/m², rod 6.1 mm. Intralox pitches it at cutting lines
  specifically: "S800 HDE in natural acetal", a "patented fault-tolerant edge design"
  that "prevents foreign material contamination (FMC) concerns", material "free of color
  additives, providing superior cut resistance", and an "open hinge design [that]
  improves cleanability"
  ([Intralox S800 HDE for meat cutting](https://www.intralox.com/media/news/upgrade-meat-cutting-lines-to-intralox-s800-hde-in-natural-acetal)).
  Note "free of color additives": the material argument for the cutting-line belt is
  about cut resistance, not colour.
- **Food-contact compliance is by polymer, and it is published.** Intralox's engineering
  manual maps each material to its clearance: PP and PE to 21 CFR 177.1520, acetal to
  177.2470, rubber-family materials to 177.2600, nylon to 177.1500, plus EU conformity
  under Reg. 1935/2004 with migration tested to Reg. 10/2011
  ([Intralox engineering manuals](https://www.intralox.com/resources/engineering-manual)).
  The one that bites for pork is **21 CFR 177.2600(f)**, the fatty-food column: extraction
  in n-hexane at reflux, not more than 175 mg/in² in the first 7 hours and 4 mg/in² in the
  succeeding 2 hours ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/section-177.2600)).
  Pork fat puts any gripper pad or belt cover in that column, not the aqueous one.
- **White for visible soil and contrast: the folk explanation does not survive checking.**
  The usual claim is that white shows soil and blood during inspection and gives contrast
  against red meat. No vendor page or engineering manual states that rationale. The
  evidence runs the other way: the same vendors sell blue and white side by side for the
  same food-contact duty (Intralox ThermoDrive in "blue, white"; Ammeraal Soliflex PRO
  ships light blue), and **metal- and X-ray-detectable belt grades exist precisely because
  colour contrast is not accepted as sufficient foreign-body control**. What is sourced is
  only that white belts are in fact used on pork inspection lines
  ([pork belly foreign-object study, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12656437/)).
  Treat the colour as an observed convention, not a specified requirement.

For the robot, the belt being white and matte is a gift: it gives high contrast against
pink and red product for segmentation, and modular belt hinge rows give a regular,
detectable texture that can be used for belt-speed estimation without an encoder. See
[depth-primary segmentation](depth-primary-segmentation.md) and
[conveyor tracking](conveyor-tracking-and-visual-servoing.md).

**Belt speed** on a pork cutting line is not published by any vendor consulted, and
Frontmatec's pace deboning line datasheet gives speed only as "VLT-speed control of belts
- individually adjustable". Three indirect bounds are sourced. Intralox's design rules
say to consider soft-start motors above **15 m/min** and add a belt-pull service factor
above **30 m/min**, which is where they expect food conveyors to sit
([engineering manuals](https://www.intralox.com/resources/engineering-manual)).
Frontmatec's Duoplan overhead conveyor tops out at 0.4 m/s (**24 m/min**)
([datasheet](https://www.frontmatec.com/media/4009/frontmatec-duoplan-conveyors_en.pdf)).
Their storage conveyor runs a standard hook pitch of **219 mm or 251 mm** at up to
1,500 pigs/h, which works out to about 5.5 m/min of chain
([datasheet](https://www.frontmatec.com/media/6367/frontmatec-storage-conveyor_en.pdf);
the m/min is derived here, not vendor-stated). So the plausible band is single-digit to
low-tens of m/min. Measure it on site rather than asking, because the answer a plant
gives is usually the nameplate and not the running value.

> the machinery or related product (if possible, in a 'cleaning' position);
> (e) machinery or related products shall be designed and constructed in such a way that
> no ancillary substances hazardous to health, including the lubricants used, can come
> into contact with foodstuffs."

Clause (e) is why NSF H1 lubricant is not optional on a robot mounted over open product.
Clause (c) is why the cell needs a defined cleaning pose. §2.1.2 additionally requires the
instructions to name cleaning products and methods "not only for easily accessible areas
but also for areas to which access is impossible or inadvisable"
([Reg. (EU) 2023/1230](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2023/1230/oj)).

### 7.3.1 US rules that constrain where a cell can physically go

These are easy to miss and expensive to discover late.

- **9 CFR 416.3(b):** "Equipment and utensils must not be constructed, located, or
  operated in a manner that prevents FSIS inspection program employees from inspecting
  the equipment or utensils to determine whether they are in sanitary condition"
  ([9 CFR 416.3](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/9/416.3)). A fully enclosed,
  hard-guarded cell that an inspector cannot see into is a regulatory problem, not only
  an engineering one.
- **9 CFR 307.2(m):** requires 5 ft of unobstructed line space per carcass inspector,
  50 foot-candles of shadow-free lighting at inspection stations, and a line control
  switch adjacent to each station
  ([9 CFR 307.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/section-307.2)). A cell cannot take
  that floor space or cast that shadow.
- **9 CFR 416.2(d):** ventilation must control "odors, vapors, and condensation to the
  extent necessary to prevent adulteration of product". A warm robot in a 10 to 12 C room
  directly over open product is a condensation source, and that is a food safety issue
  before it is a camera-fogging issue.
- **9 CFR 310.26(c):** under NSIS, "the line speed limits in § 310.1 do not apply to the
  establishment, provided it is able to maintain effective process control"
  ([eCFR](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-9/section-310.26)).

### 7.4 What a robot has to survive at washdown

The night sanitation shift is the design load, not the production shift. Watch
[Meat Institute, Sanitation in Meat and Poultry Plants](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GUJ4vI-JA88)
before specifying anything.

**Ingress protection.** IP69K is the de facto bar for guarding and end-effector housings
in wet protein plants. It is defined in **ISO 20653:2023** (24 pp, ISO/TC 22/SC 32,
itself now at stage 90.92 to be revised), not in IEC 60529, whose current form is
IEC 60529:1989+AMD1:1999+AMD2:2013 CSV. **The numeric test parameters could not be
verified.** Both standards are paywalled and every route to their text (iso.org OBP,
ANSI, SAI Global, DIN Media, SIS) refused this network. The figures that circulate in
trade literature (80 C water, 80 to 100 bar, 14 to 16 L/min, four nozzle angles, 30 s
each, on a turntable) were **not confirmed against ISO 20653 or DIN 40050-9. Do not put
them in a specification on the strength of this note.** ISO 20653:2023 is 24 pages. Buy
it.

Note also that **IP69K does not imply IP67**. The second IP numeral is a ladder only to 6;
7 and 8 are a separate immersion branch, which is why dual markings like IP66/IP67 exist.
IP69K is a high-momentum, hot, short-duration close-range jet test; IP67 is sustained
static immersion. A gasket that shrugs off a jet can still breathe water in during an
immersion cool-down. (That reasoning is engineering inference, not a sourced claim,
because the definitions themselves are paywalled.)

**Washdown-rated robots, from vendor pages.** These claims are verifiable, so here they
are rather than a hand-wave.

| Robot | Payload / reach | Protection | Food-specific claims |
|---|---|---|---|

Three cautions. FANUC's body-versus-wrist split rating is the norm, not the exception, so
read the whole datasheet rather than the headline number. Stäubli's HE payload table and
its own pork-deboning case page disagree (table says TX2-60 at 4.5 kg, the case page says
5 to 9 kg), so confirm before sizing a tool. And the three vendors' lubricant wording
differs materially: FANUC says "NSF H1 food-grade grease", ABB says "NSF H1 Food Grade Oil
throughout", Stäubli says "NSF H1 compliant food oil". H1 means **incidental** contact
only, limited to 10 ppm in product under 21 CFR 178.3570, not direct food contact
([Mobil SHC Cibus](https://www.mobil.com/en/lubricants/for-businesses/industrial/lubricants/product-series/mobil-shc-cibus-series)).

**Chemicals.** Caustic foam, rinse, acid foam, applied hot. Vendor technical datasheets
were not obtainable, so the numbers below come from peer-reviewed plant studies rather
than from a chemical supplier: a chlorine-based cleaning and degreasing foam used in
Canadian meat plants applied at **2.5% v/v, 60 C, 5 min contact**, against a baseline of
"high-pressure, hot water (60 °C) sprays" on an overnight shift following two 8-hour
fabrication shifts
([doi:10.3389/fmicb.2026.1818470](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmicb.2026.1818470)); and a
cleanability benchmark of **1.28% NaOH at 66 C for 3 min**, rinse, neutralise in 0.1%
phosphoric acid for 30 s, sanitise at 100 ppm hypochlorite
([doi:10.4315/0362-028x-64.8.1178](https://doi.org/10.4315/0362-028x-64.8.1178)).

**Anodised aluminium is the wrong material here.** Anodic Al₂O₃ is amphoteric and
dissolves in alkali ([doi:10.1016/S0013-4686(98)00368-5](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0013-4686(98)00368-5)).
A specific pH threshold for onset of attack is unverified, but the design conclusion does
not depend on one: do not put an anodised housing or gripper frame in a plant running
daily alkaline foam, whatever its IP rating.

**The elastomer problem has no single answer.** From Parker's O-Ring Handbook
compatibility ratings (1 satisfactory, 4 unsatisfactory):

| Medium | NBR | HNBR | EPDM | FKM |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Caustic soda | 3 | 3 | **1** | 3 |
| Capric acid (C10, the closest table proxy to rendered pork fat) | **1** | **1** | **4** | **1** |

That is the whole seal and gripper-pad problem in two rows. **EPDM wins on hot caustic and
loses on fat; NBR and FKM do the reverse.** No single common elastomer covers a gripper
that handles pork fat all shift and takes hot alkaline foam all night. Either qualify
HNBR or a peroxide-cured EPDM against the plant's actual chemistry, or design the pad as a
sanitation-schedule consumable and price the replacements. EHEDG GL 48 is the guideline to
buy for this.

**Stainless.** 316L over 304 wherever chlorides are present, which on a meat floor is
everywhere. PREN (= %Cr + 3.3(%Mo + 0.5%W) + 16%N) is 18 to 20 for 304 and 24 to 26 for
316, with the L grades mattering because frames get welded
([IMOA, Practical Guidelines for the Fabrication of Austenitic Stainless Steels, Table 1](https://www.imoa.info/download_files/stainless-steel/Austenitics.pdf)).
IMOA's caveats matter more than the number: PREN "should only be used as an indicator of
an alloy's potential performance", and the named failure conditions are "built-in crevices
like bolted joints" and "careless fabrication practices ... creating roughly ground
surfaces where corrosion can initiate". That is the same design decision EHEDG's ban on
metal-to-metal joints points at, arriving from the corrosion side.

**On Ra below 0.8 micrometres: the number is folklore.** It is quoted everywhere and no
standards clause could be found behind it. EN 1672-2:2020, ISO 14159:2002 and EHEDG GL 8
are all paywalled and unopenable, so the possibility it sits in one of them cannot be
excluded. What is checkable points elsewhere:

- EFSA opinions that discuss surface roughness as a hygienic design criterion and cite
  EN 1672-2, 2006/42/EC and ISO 14159:2002 state **no Ra value at all**
  ([doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2024.8521](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2024.8521),
  [doi:10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6092](https://doi.org/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6092)).
- The traceable numbers are different numbers and all explicitly non-mandatory: ASTM
  A480/A480M No. 4 finish "may be up to 0.64 µm (25 µin) but this is not a mandated
  limit"; No. 3 up to 1.0 µm, also not mandated; EN 10088 2K "transverse Ra less than
  0.5 µm" (IMOA Table 29, same PDF). 0.8 µm matches neither. The likeliest origin is
  3-A's 32 µin, which is 0.813 µm, but no 3-A text could be opened to confirm it.
- The cleanability evidence for the threshold is weak. Surfaces from Ra 23 nm to 900 nm
  sprayed with *P. aeruginosa* or *S. aureus* and detergent-cleaned showed a 2-log
  reduction overall and **no significant difference in cleanability between them**
  (P > 0.05) ([doi:10.4315/0362-028x-64.8.1183](https://doi.org/10.4315/0362-028x-64.8.1183)).
  A companion AFM study found defect *shape* dominates: "sharp scratches [are] more
  difficult to clean than wider" features
  ([doi:10.4315/0362-028x-64.1.87](https://doi.org/10.4315/0362-028x-64.1.87)). Ra
  averages away exactly the sharp directional scratches that retain soil. A 2006 review
  argues the polished-surface practice "is a misconception, that it provides no real
  benefit" (Riedewald, PDA J Pharm Sci Technol 60(3):164-171, PMID 17089685).
- Yaskawa nonetheless prints "surface roughness < 0.8 Ra" as an HD-series spec, which is
  itself evidence the figure circulates as a de facto procurement requirement rather than
  a standards clause.

**Practical recommendation:** specify the finish designation (ASTM A480 No. 4 or EN 10088
2K) plus a measured transverse Ra with a stated cut-off and instrument, and add a
directionality and defect requirement. A bare "Ra < 0.8 µm" is unenforceable and does not
buy what people think it buys.

**Design.** No horizontal ledges, no crevices, no hollow sections that can hold water,
drainable in the installed cleaning pose, all fasteners accessible, and no bolted joint in
the product zone if EL Class I certification is wanted. This dominates cell design more
than the robot choice does.

**Practical consequence.** Either buy a washdown-rated robot from the table above, or put
a standard robot in a sealed, positively pressurised sleeve and certify the enclosure
rather than the robot. Both are in use. Whichever route, check it against 9 CFR 416.3(b)
first: an enclosure an inspector cannot see into is its own problem.

## 8. Where automation has and has not succeeded

### 8.1 The honest position

Pork deboning is one of the least automated operations in industrial food production.
The reason is not that nobody has tried. The peer-reviewed framing:

> "There is a strong demand for automation to solve these problems, but it is still
> challenging in the whole meat process automation due to the high product variability,
> dexterous manipulation, harsh environment, and space constraints. Compared with other
> industries, the meat industry's working environment is not very conducive to robotics.
> Meat processing automation is constrained by equipment sensitivity to size variations
> and material deformability, necessitating adaptive robotics."
> ([Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2025, doi:10.3389/frobt.2025.1578318](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12141337))

The Danish Meat Research Institute, who build this equipment, publish the numbers that
matter more than any capability claim
([Hinrichsen, Wu & Gregersen 2022, *Animal Frontiers* 12(2), doi:10.1093/af/vfab077](https://doi.org/10.1093/af/vfab077)):

- "poultry processors can handle 20,000 carcasses per hour, pig processors 750 per hour,
  and beef processors 300 per hour"
- "**An uptime less than 80% is not uncommon and sometimes even as low as 50%.**"
- "the utilization of the automation technology is low and **may be only 30% of the full
  capacity**"
- "The large biological variation and variations introduced from upstream processing by
  **human operators or machines** make this task extremely challenging"

Read that third bullet twice before promising a payback period. And note the second half
of the fourth: upstream machines and people inject variation *on top of* the animal's own,
so a cell's input distribution is worse than the biology alone would suggest. DMRI's own
demonstrator cell does toe cutting, tenderloin extraction, ear cutting, stab wound
removal, head cutting and trisection. **No deboning, no seaming.**

A scale threshold worth checking against the customer's plant: one review concludes "full
automation is feasible only in facilities processing more than 600 heads per hour (about
25,000 heads per week)"
([Bae et al. 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9951719/)).

Unpack the difficulty into the four real blockers:

1. **Biological variation.** No two legs are the same shape, and the bones inside are not
   where a CAD model says they are. Every cut is referenced to anatomy that must be found
   on the piece, not assumed.
2. **Yield.** A human deboner leaves a known amount of meat on the bone. A machine that
   leaves more destroys the business case, because the ham primal is high value and bone
   is only 9.6% of it. A 1% yield loss across a plant is larger than the labour saving.
3. **Blades near compliance.** The cut has to follow a surface that moves when you push
   on it. Force control and cut-path adaptation are both required, at the same time.
4. **Hygiene and washdown.** Every mechanism has to survive section 7.4 every night.

### 8.2 What has succeeded

- **Whole-carcass operations with a strong pose prior.** Scalding, dehairing, singeing,
  splitting, grading. The carcass hangs on a rail; gravity and the rail fix the pose.
- **Slaughter-line robots on hanging carcasses.** Bung dropping, aitch bone cutting,
  belly opening, neck clipping, marking
  ([Frontmatec AiRA](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/clean-line-chill-room/aira-robots/)).
- **Primal separation by measurement.** AGOL measures each carcass then cuts it
  ([Frontmatec](https://www.frontmatec.com/en/pork-solutions/primal-cutting/automatic-primal-cutting/));
  JBT Marel's Primal Cutter runs up to 600 carcasses per hour
  ([JBT Marel](https://jbtmarel.com/en/meat/pig-pork/cutting-deboning-and-trimming/)).
- **Pulling instead of cutting.** Loin pullers, rib pullers, blade bone pullers. Where a
  natural seam exists, exploit it mechanically rather than trying to cut along it.
- **One full deboner.** Mayekawa HAMDAS-RX, 500 hams/hour maximum, described in the
  literature as "the world's first automated ham-deboning robotic system"
  ([Mayekawa](https://www.mayekawa.com/products/deboning_machines/),
  [Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2025](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12141337)).
  It works on a **pre-cut** leg with the hipbone already off, so someone still prepares
  and presents the piece. Its measured performance is the yardstick for anything
  proposed on this line:

  | Metric | Value | Source |
  |---|---|---|
  | Yield, skin-off fresh ham (prototype) | **86.64%** | [ICoMST 1999](https://digicomst.ie/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/1999_02_03.pdf) |
  | Yield, skin-on fresh ham (prototype) | **88.23%** | same |
  | Residual muscle left between tibia and splint bone | about **15 g** | same |
  | Average meat loss, production RX | **60 g per ham part** | [Bae et al. 2023](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani13040651) |
  | Cycle, prototype | 12 s (10 s dwell, 2 s transfer), 300 hams/h, 17 stations | [ICoMST 1999](https://digicomst.ie/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/1999_02_03.pdf) |
  | Machine mass, production RX | 13,500 kg | [spec sheet](https://americas.mayekawa.com/mna/downloads/pdf/Food%20Robotics/HAMDAS.pdf) |

  Three design choices in that machine are worth copying. It **measures every piece**,
  twice. It uses **X-ray to find the thigh bone** rather than assuming where it is. And
  it **peels rather than cuts** where it can, "which results in less cutting damage"
  (spec sheet, above). Mayekawa's European datasheet additionally describes a force
  sensor identifying cutting torque under feedback control; that page would not serve
  over TLS from this network, so the force-control claim is **unverified**.
- **A knife-wielding robot cell in pork that actually exists.** Mayekawa's CELLDAS uses
  "three Stäubli six-axis robots, including TX2-60 HE and TX2-90 HE models. While two of
  the robots are responsible for handling and positioning the pieces of pork, the third
  six-axis robot, equipped with a knife, performs the deboning." It combines a 3D scan
  with X-ray, then "the control system creates a plan for butchering with the optimal
  cutting paths". Cycle is **around 40 seconds** for hip bone and tailbone
  ([Stäubli, deboning of pork](https://www.staubli.com/global/en/robotics/industries/food/protein-processing/deboning-of-pork.html)).
  The sentence on that page worth pinning above a mechanical designer's desk: "The knife
  on the robot arm can unexpectedly hit bones in the meat, become jammed in the bone, and
  suddenly release. This means that **strong and irregular torques are exerted on the
  drivetrain**." Two handling robots to one cutting robot is also a useful ratio: even in
  a cutting cell, most of the robots are doing presentation.
- **Tool sterilisation as an engineered feature.** JBT Marel's M-Line TwinTool runs two
  tools so that "while one tool is in operation another similar tool is being sterilized
  inside a cabinet at the head of the robot. Thus, no manual sterilization is needed"
  ([M-Line Bung Remover](https://jbtmarel.com/en/products/m-line-bung-remover-mbr/)).
  Frontmatec's AiRA cells similarly list a "robot with hygienic protective cover" and a
  "sterilization and cleaning unit" with "automatic cleaning and disinfection of tools
  after each process". In deployed meat cells, the distinctive hygiene mechanism is tool
  sterilisation between pieces, not force limiting.
- **Other species.** Scott Technology's x-ray-guided lamb primal system at JBS Bordertown
  is the flagship deployed robotic meat cell
  ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BLrfJ0UvPhg)), processing carcasses at a stated
  10 per minute through six machines beginning with x-ray grading
  ([Scott automated boning room](https://scottautomation.com/en/products/meat/automated-boning-room);
  note Scott's lamb page states 12 per minute for the same system, so the figure is soft).
  The review notes their beef system "can reduce workloads of two to three per shift"
  ([Frontiers in Robotics and AI 2025](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12141337)).
  For blade safety on the human side of the same floor, Scott's BladeStop bandsaw is
  "capable of stopping in <10ms" and is DGUV certified for functional safety
  ([BladeStop](https://scottautomation.com/en/bladestop)).

### 8.3 What has not

- **Evisceration.** Still overwhelmingly a knife and a pair of hands.
- **General deboning.** [StreamLine](https://jbtmarel.com/en/products/streamline/),
  [PaceLine](https://jbtmarel.com/en/products/paceline/pig-pork/) and
  [DeboFlex](https://jbtmarel.com/en/products/deboflex) are all *operator* lines. They
  automate the conveying, pacing, weighing and traceability, and leave the knife to a
  person. That is the honest state of the art for pork deboning: automate everything
  around the cut, not the cut.
- **Trimming to specification.** Robotic belly trimmers exist
  ([Frontmatec](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=r_YVzofAl_A)) but trimming a leg to a
  fat-cover specification is still hand work.
- **Presentation and orientation.** Which is precisely why the customer's operators are
  standing there doing it by hand.
- **Hindquarter deboning, which was installed and then removed.** This is the case study
  to keep in mind. Four hindquarter deboning machines were running when "the workers
  alongside them ... took up the challenge to improve their own performance. Within weeks,
  their yield had improved to a level that was not attainable in the machines." The
  machines were taken out of production
  ([Seaton 2022, *Animal Frontiers* 12(2), doi:10.1093/af/vfac022](https://doi.org/10.1093/af/vfac022)).
  The same paper gives the cost ratio that decides these arguments: "the machinery can
  cost 5 to 10 times the yearly saving in labor."
- **Fenceless or collaborative meat cutting.** Food-grade cobot hardware exists (FANUC
  CRX Food Grade, Yaskawa HC-series with food-grade grease as standard), and one research
  group has run a UR10 with a knife on pork loin with expert graders rating cuts fair or
  good roughly 70% of the time across 23 slicing, 26 trimming and 27 cubing replicates
  ([Wright et al. 2024, *Sci Rep* 14, doi:10.1038/s41598-023-50569-4](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-50569-4)).
  But **no vendor claims a deployed fenceless power-and-force-limited meat cutting cell.**
  Every knife-carrying cell that could be sourced is fenced and interlocked. Treat
  collaborative meat cutting as unproven.

### 8.3.1 The research frontier, and how far it is from a line

RoBUTCHER is the best-funded recent attempt at a robotic meat factory cell: EUR 7.54 M of
EU contribution, January 2020 to June 2023, coordinated by NMBU with Animalia and DMRI
([CORDIS 871631](https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/871631)). It reached **TRL 6**,
prototype in a relevant environment, for hot and cold cutting, and found "a gap in tooling
for robots working with meat". Its published cycle on whole unchilled carcasses was
**8 to 12 minutes per carcass**
([Alvseike et al. 2020, *Acta Vet Scand* 62:47, doi:10.1186/s13028-020-00546-y](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13028-020-00546-y);
platform paper [Mason et al. 2024, *IJRR*, doi:10.1177/02783649241234035](https://doi.org/10.1177/02783649241234035)).
Against a 750 pigs/hour line, which is 4.8 s per carcass, that is the size of the gap
between the research frontier and production.

Two outputs of that programme are directly usable rather than merely interesting. The
adaptive cutting method aligns a CT-derived 3D model to a RealSense point cloud via
Bayesian Coherent Point Drift and generates trajectories from a cutting-surface model
built around skeletal structure
([doi:10.1016/j.atech.2023.100388](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.atech.2023.100388)). And
there is a **public RGB-D pig carcass cutting dataset**: 25 pigs, six RealSense D415 in an
inward 360-degree rig, then a single robot-mounted camera cycling the same poses, captured
cut by cut through a seven-step sequence
([Esper et al. 2022, *Data in Brief*, doi:10.1016/j.dib.2022.107945](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dib.2022.107945)).
That is the closest public data to this cell's perception problem and it is worth pulling
before collecting anything.

One gripper result to design against. A sensor-equipped meat gripper with a maximum
clamping force of about **60 N** on a 3 cm object measured **over 350 N during trachea
extraction** on a real pig carcass, identified as the tensile strength of the trachea
([Takács et al. 2024, *Sensors* 24(14):4631, doi:10.3390/s24144631](https://doi.org/10.3390/s24144631)).
The same authors rule out soft grippers for this industry because "their (currently)
relatively low payload and difficult disinfection prevent their direct utilization", and
say the job needs "strong and powerful tools" for "whole legs of pigs or cattle". Design
the gripper for the forces the *product* can generate, not for the nominal payload.

The economics paper says the same thing from the money side: automation "require[s]
substantial capital investment and may reduce flexibility in responding to market shifts
or specification changes", so "the economic viability of automation depends on scale,
stability of fabrication specifications, and long-term labor cost expectations"
([Transl Anim Sci 2026](https://europepmc.org/article/PMC/PMC12978301)).

### 8.4 The economics, from someone who funded twenty years of it

Meat & Livestock Australia's account of their own programme is the most useful document
in this note for framing a pitch
([Ruberg 2021, *J Applied Business and Economics* 23(4):257-270, doi:10.33423/jabe.v23i4.4480](https://doi.org/10.33423/jabe.v23i4.4480),
[PDF](https://articlegateway.com/journals/index.php/JABE/article/download/4480/4259)).

Named failures, verbatim: "CSIRO's 'Fututech' automated beef slaughter (circa 1990), and
automated lamb hindquarter deboning (circa 2005) ... failed to deliver practical
cost-effective solutions. They demonstrated technical feasibility, even engineering
virtuosity, but delivered no industry innovation or impact." And: "the industry is
littered with gloriously courageous but failed early attempts."

What success cost: automated lamb primal middle boning took "Inputs: $40M over 15 years"
to produce "A cost effective reliable system ... for $4M which adds $6/carcase value at 10
carcases per minute". "**Three generations** of lamb boning automation were required."

**Where the money actually comes from, which is the single most important sentence here:**

> "the benefits from automation do not come from labour savings (ie; only around 15%),
> while **carcase value yield upswing benefits represent 80% of the gains** (ie; precise
> cutting maximising $25/kg product versus $3/kg product spanning across the cutting
> line)."

Investment heuristics from the same paper: "Sensing is the most important enabler for any
task automation"; a viable solution "must be: more accurate than the current human
process, and provide yield improvements, and or redeploy more than five labour units per
installation"; and "Solution providers of fewer than 50 FTEs and or less than $30 million
turnover have no success recorded in finalising and commercialising processing
technology."

Two warnings that bear directly on this project. Transfer does not work: "Solutions that
appeared effective for **European pork production ultimately failed to transfer** to beef
and lamb processing." And on the frontier: "the whole area of meat from bone separation,
and meat muscle seaming separation and product trimming, is currently technologically out
of reach and 'next' generation, requiring substantial university level basic research."

One caution on a number that gets repeated: Ruberg writes that DMRI "ascertained that a 1%
gain in gross revenue from more precise cutting is worth as much as a 12.5% reduction in
wages", but his endnote points to Hobbs, Kerr & Klein (1998), which is not a DMRI
publication. **Attribution unverified; do not quote it as DMRI's.**

### 8.5 One case study with numbers attached

The best public before-and-after on a pork deboning floor is JBT Marel's Alibem
installation in Brazil: **4,000 animals per day**, an **80% reduction in bone-in meat
waste** against the previous conventional deboning process, and operator work rate down
from **60+ actions per minute** on the manual line to about **20 actions per minute** on
the automated hanging line, against a legal maximum of 30
([JBT Marel, Alibem](https://jbtmarel.com/en/customer-stories/alibem-automates-deboning-and-raises-yield-with-deboflex/)).

Read that carefully. It is a vendor case study, so treat the percentages as marketing.
But the shape of the claim is instructive and matches everything else in this section:
**the win came from presentation, pacing and positioning, not from automating the cut.**
The knives were still held by people. That is exactly the intervention the customer is
asking for at the leg station.

## 9. Plausible automation targets, ranked

Ranked by tractability for a **first** robot cell, most tractable first. The ranking
criterion is: how much of the task can be done without referencing internal anatomy, and
how cheap is a failure.

**Before the list, the arithmetic that decides whether any of it is worth funding.** The
business case for meat robotics is cut accuracy, not labour. Worked examples for the three
species ([Khodabandehloo 2022, *Animal Frontiers* 12(2), doi:10.1093/af/vfac012](https://doi.org/10.1093/af/vfac012)):

| Species | Claim |
|---|---|
| Pork | "A 5 mm cut position improvement, at 20 US cents/mm ... was estimated to achieve over US$ 300k per year" at 180 carcasses/h, single shift |
| Lamb | "For 2 cuts at 600 carcasses per hour ... the overall gain is US$ 367,200 for a **single mm** position optimization" |
| Beef | "At a nominal 5 mm in favor of the higher value primal ... this is close to US$ 350,000/year" at 400 head/h |

And the hurdle it has to clear: "Commercial systems need to target an **ROI of 18-24
months** for users to be motivated to invest." Combine that with DMRI's 50 to 80% uptime
and 30% utilisation (section 8.1) and the conclusion is uncomfortable but clear: a cell
justified purely on replacing one operator will not pay back, and a cell that is down 20%
of the time will not either. Placement accuracy that protects downstream cut position is
where the money is, which happens to be exactly what this station does.

**1. Leg presentation and orientation onto the cutter infeed.** This is the customer's
ask and it is correctly chosen. It requires pose estimation of a rigid-enough object, a
pick, and a place into a fixed frame. It does not require cutting, does not require
finding a bone, and a failure means a mis-oriented leg that a person can correct. The
trotter gives a high-contrast, low-variance grasp feature, and the white belt gives
clean segmentation. Risk concentrates in the placement tolerance at the cutter infeed,
which is the number to nail down first. Takt to beat: a human at this station is doing
roughly 20 to 60 actions per minute depending on line design (section 4.3), and the
machine downstream of a comparable station runs a 7.2 s cycle per leg.

**2. Singulation and de-clumping on the infeed belt.** Separating legs that arrive
touching or overlapping, so that (1) always sees one object. Failure mode is benign, the
sensing is the same sensing, and it can be built as an extension of (1) rather than a
separate cell.

**3. Weight-band sorting and diverting after grading.** Legs are already weighed. Moving
a leg into one of N lanes by weight is a pick-and-place with a discrete target and no
precision requirement. Lowest technical risk of anything on this list, but also the
lowest value, because pushers and ARB belts already do it without a robot
([Intralox ARB transfer at a meat plant](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=7tZG7_XREfY)).

**4. Bin and dolav loading, and rack loading for the salting room.** Heavy, repetitive,
poor ergonomics, no precision requirement beyond "in the bin". Attractive on labour
grounds, unattractive because payload and reach push toward a large robot and the safety
case gets expensive.

**5. Hanging-line hook loading.** Putting a leg onto a hook on a hanging deboning line.
Harder than (1) because the target is moving and the insertion is a real assembly task
against a compliant object, but it is still not cutting.

**6. Shank or hock cut.** A single straight cut at a joint, referenced to a feature that
is externally visible. Tractable in principle, but now there is a blade, and the safety
and hygiene case steps up sharply.

**7. Trimming to a fat-cover specification.** Requires surface-following force control
and a yield-aware cut policy. Real research problem.

**8. Full deboning.** Do not. Mayekawa presented a working 300 legs/hour prototype at
ICoMST in 1999 and the production machine still requires the hipbone removed beforehand,
still needs preparation and rework operators, and caps at 500 hams per hour on a 13.5 t
machine. That is more than twenty-five years of sustained engineering by a company that
does nothing else, for a partial solution.

The cell described in [meat cell architecture](meat-cell-architecture.md) targets (1),
which is the right call. Targets (2) and (5) are the natural second and third increments
on the same hardware.

## 10. Open questions for the customer

Answering these changes the design more than any amount of further reading.

1. What is the cutter infeed placement tolerance, in mm and degrees? Everything else
   follows from this.
2. What is the takt: legs per hour per lane, and how many lanes?
3. Which route are these legs on: dry-cure, cooked ham, or export fresh? That determines
   whether the trotter stays on and therefore the grasp feature.
4. Can they export the grading weigher histogram for a week? That gives the mass
   distribution for free.
5. Belt speed, measured not nameplate, and belt series and pitch.
6. Product temperature at the station, measured at the deep ham and at the surface.
7. Washdown procedure: chemicals, temperature, pressure, duration, and whether the cell
   area is hosed directly or only wiped.
8. Is there space above the belt for an overhead mount, and what is the drainage under
   the proposed cell footprint?
